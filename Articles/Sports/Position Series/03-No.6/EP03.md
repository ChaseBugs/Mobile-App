# SERIES 03 — The Evolution of the No.6

# EP03 — The Busquets Revolution

## How Did Sergio Busquets Change the Definition of the No.6?

Sergio Busquets was not fast.

He was not an explosive dribbler.

He was not known for long-range goals.

And yet, during the peak years of Barcelona and Spain, he was almost always at the centre of the team.

Why?

Because Busquets did not simply try to beat pressure.

He learned how to **use pressure itself**.

Makélélé protected the balance of the team.

Busquets pushed the role in a different direction.

From:

**Protect the Team**

to:

**Control the Press.**

---

# 1. Busquets Did Not Avoid Pressure

Most players see pressure as danger.

An opponent approaches.

They release the ball quickly.

Space disappears.

They play backwards.

Busquets often behaved differently.

Sometimes, he allowed the opponent to come closer.

```text
Opponent
   ↓

Busquets ●

   ↓
Wait
```

Why?

Because when one defender moves toward Busquets, space opens somewhere else.

```text
Opponent → Busquets

          ↓

      Free Teammate
```

For Busquets, pressing was not only a threat.

It could also become a tool for creating space.

---

# 2. His Main Skill Was Not Escape — It Was Attraction

When people hear **Press Resistance**, they often imagine this:

```text
PRESS
  ↓
DRIBBLE
  ↓
ESCAPE
```

Busquets often solved the same problem differently.

```text
PRESS
  ↓
ATTRACT
  ↓
RELEASE
```

Draw the opponent in.

Wait.

Release the ball at the right moment.

Once the defender commits, another space becomes available.

This was one of Busquets' core mechanisms:

**Attract Pressure → Free Space → Progress**

---

# 3. The Important Action Happened Before the First Touch

Busquets' play started before the ball arrived.

He constantly scanned.

Left.

Right.

Behind.

Forward again.

```text
SCAN
 ↓
SCAN
 ↓
SCAN
 ↓
RECEIVE
```

That meant he often knew the next action before touching the ball.

An average player may operate like this:

```text
Receive
 ↓
Look
 ↓
Think
 ↓
Pass
```

Busquets often operated like this:

```text
Look
 ↓
Think
 ↓
Receive
 ↓
Pass
```

That difference created time under pressure.

His biggest advantage was not quick feet.

It was **quick thinking**.

---

# 4. Body Orientation Was One of His Main Weapons

Busquets could escape pressure without spectacular dribbling.

Body orientation was critical.

Receive with a closed body:

```text
CB → BUSQUETS

      PRESS ↑

No forward view
```

Now the options are limited.

Receive with an open body:

```text
CB → BUSQUETS
          ↘
            FORWARD
```

Now the next pass can happen immediately.

Busquets did not receive the ball and then search for space.

He prepared his body toward the solution before the ball arrived.

That was a major part of his Press Resistance.

---

# 5. He Could Eliminate Two Players Without Dribbling Past One

A dribbler may beat one defender directly.

Busquets could remove multiple opponents with one pass.

```text
   ●     ●

      BUSQUETS
          ↓
        PASS
          ↓
        No.8
```

Two players step toward him.

He releases the ball behind them.

Now both pressers are out of the immediate action.

```text
2 PRESSERS
    ↓
OUT OF PLAY
```

He did not beat them physically.

He beat them with:

**Position + Timing + Pass**

---

# 6. One of His Greatest Weapons Was the Pause

Football values speed.

But playing quickly does not mean playing immediately every time.

Busquets often waited for a fraction of a second.

Half a second.

One second.

That small pause changed the defensive structure.

```text
Receive
 ↓
PAUSE
 ↓
Opponent Steps
 ↓
Pass
```

The pause was not passive.

It forced the opponent to make a decision.

Busquets often moved the defender before moving the ball.

---

# 7. Passing Was About Manipulation, Not Just Accuracy

Describing Busquets as an accurate passer is not enough.

The important question is:

**What did the pass cause the opponent to do?**

Imagine:

```text
CB → Busquets
```

The opponent shifts toward the centre.

Busquets then passes sideways.

```text
Busquets → RB
```

On paper, it looks like a simple sideways pass.

But if the midfield has already moved toward Busquets, the right-back now receives in more space.

So the real purpose was not only:

**Move the Ball.**

It was:

**Move the Opponent.**

That is Busquets-style playmaking.

---

# 8. He Manipulated Defenders as Much as the Ball

A good playmaker moves the ball.

A great one can also move the defence.

Busquets often attracted both.

```text
BALL
 ↓
BUSQUETS

OPPONENT
   ↓
BUSQUETS
```

Then he released the ball away from the pressure.

```text
PRESS HERE
   ↓

BUSQUETS
   ↓
PASS → FREE SIDE
```

One action changed two things.

The position of the ball.

And the position of the opponent.

---

# 9. Third-Man Football Was Essential to His Game

Busquets did not always need to play the final progressive pass himself.

Sometimes:

```text
CB → Busquets → No.8
```

But Barcelona's positional structures often used a third player.

```text
A → B → C
```

A passes to B.

B connects to C.

But C was always the real target.

For example:

```text
Busquets → Xavi
            ↓
          Messi
```

Busquets understood these relationships extremely well.

He could help break pressure without making the spectacular final pass.

He opened the first door.

---

# 10. La Pausa — When Waiting Becomes an Attacking Action

Busquets rarely looked rushed.

He received.

The opponent approached.

He waited.

```text
BUSQUETS
   ↓
WAIT
   ↓
DEFENDER COMMITS
   ↓
PASS
```

That delay forced the defender to choose.

Press?

Hold?

Cover?

If the defender pressed, space opened behind.

If he did not, Busquets had time to turn.

That gave the opponent a dilemma.

---

# 11. Not Pressing Him Was Also Dangerous

If you press Busquets, space opens.

So why not leave him alone?

That creates another problem.

```text
DON'T PRESS
   ↓
BUSQUETS
   ↓
TURN
   ↓
FIND FREE SIDE
```

Now he has time to dictate the next phase.

So the defensive problem becomes:

```text
PRESS BUSQUETS
     ↓
SPACE OPENS


DON'T PRESS
     ↓
HE CONTROLS
```

This is why he was so difficult to deal with.

He did not simply survive pressure.

He made the decision to press him a tactical problem.

---

# 12. He Replaced Speed With Space

Busquets was never an explosive runner.

But he was rarely late.

Why?

Because he read the game earlier.

```text
FAST PLAYER

See late
 ↓
Run fast


BUSQUETS

See early
 ↓
Move less
```

This is one of the most important lessons from his game.

Physical speed is valuable.

But early information can reduce the need for it.

**Anticipation can replace Acceleration.**

---

# 13. Small Movements Broke Big Presses

Busquets did not usually escape pressure with long carries.

His movements were often tiny.

One step.

A half-turn.

A shoulder drop.

A sole touch.

```text
PRESS →
      BUSQUETS
          ↘
       Small Turn
```

A small change in angle could completely remove the presser from the action.

This was less about classic dribbling and more about:

**Pressure Manipulation.**

He did not need to run into space.

He changed the angle of the pressure.

---

# 14. Busquets Inside Barcelona's Positional Play

To understand Busquets, you need to understand his position inside Barcelona's structure.

```text
        ST

LW      AM      RW

    XAVI   INIESTA

      BUSQUETS

LB    CB      CB    RB
```

He operated at the base of midfield.

But he was not simply staying behind the attack.

He connected the lines.

Receive from the centre-backs.

Find Xavi or Iniesta.

Switch play when the press became narrow.

Create a new passing angle when another player moved.

He was not central because he dominated possession individually.

He was central because so many connections passed through him.

---

# 15. His Position Helped Organise Everyone Else

When Busquets occupied the correct zone, teammates could position themselves more aggressively.

Xavi could move higher.

Iniesta could enter the half-space.

Full-backs could advance.

Centre-backs could spread wider.

```text
         XAVI ↑

    INIESTA ↑

      BUSQUETS

CB ←             → CB
```

This is different from Makélélé.

Makélélé often protected the risk created by attacking movement.

Busquets helped stabilise the **possession structure itself**.

That distinction matters.

---

# 16. He Defended With Possession

Busquets' defensive value cannot be explained only through tackles.

If Barcelona kept the ball:

the opponent could not counter.

The opponent had to keep shifting.

The number of defensive transitions decreased.

```text
KEEP BALL
   ↓
CONTROL SPACE
   ↓
REDUCE TRANSITIONS
   ↓
LESS DEFENDING
```

This is a major tactical principle.

Possession can also be a defensive tool.

Busquets helped protect the team while the team had the ball.

---

# 17. He Prepared for Defence Before the Ball Was Lost

Busquets did not only react after possession changed.

His position during the attack already prepared the team for a possible turnover.

```text
POSSESSION
   ↓
POSITION
   ↓
BALL LOST
   ↓
ALREADY READY
```

This position served two purposes.

Build-up.

And immediate protection after losing possession.

This is closely connected to the modern concept of:

**Rest Defence.**

A team should already have defensive security while attacking.

---

# 18. Interception Explained Him Better Than Tackling

Busquets could tackle.

But interceptions often represented his style better.

Read the pass.

Step into the lane.

Recover the ball.

Immediately connect the next action.

```text
READ
 ↓
STEP
 ↓
INTERCEPT
 ↓
PASS
```

The key is that the defensive action did not end with winning the ball.

The next attack began immediately.

For Busquets, defending and building were often part of the same sequence.

---

# 19. Busquets' Defensive Intelligence

His defensive intelligence can be reduced to three ideas.

### Position Before Duel

Solve the problem with positioning before physical contact becomes necessary.

### Read the Passing Lane

Predict where the opponent wants to play next.

### Control Space

Prioritise the dangerous zone, not simply the location of the ball.

```text
SPACE
 ↓
READ
 ↓
POSITION
 ↓
INTERCEPT
```

He was not great because he won endless duels.

He was great because he prevented many duels from becoming necessary.

---

# 20. Short Passes Could Still Be Progressive

There was once a common stereotype about defensive midfielders.

They only played safe sideways passes.

Busquets helped change that perception.

His passes were often short.

But their effect could still be progressive.

```text
SHORT PASS
   ↓
BREAK PRESS
   ↓
NEW ANGLE
   ↓
PROGRESS
```

Progression is not defined by distance.

A five-metre pass can destroy an entire pressing structure.

That was one of Busquets' most important lessons.

---

# 21. He Often Created the Action Before the Assist

Busquets was not usually the final creator.

But many attacks began with him.

```text
BUSQUETS
   ↓
XAVI
   ↓
MESSI
   ↓
CHANCE
```

The scoresheet may not show his contribution.

But without the first pass through pressure, the later action may never happen.

He often created the phase before the creative phase.

You could call it:

**Pre-Creation.**

---

# 22. Why Did His Football Look So Easy?

Busquets often looked simple.

One touch.

Short pass.

Turn.

Recycle.

Why?

Because he solved problems early.

```text
BAD PLAYER

Problem appears
   ↓
Solve problem


BUSQUETS

Read problem
   ↓
Prevent problem
```

He rarely waited for situations to become chaotic.

He acted before they did.

That is why his football sometimes looked easy.

The action was simple because the thinking behind it was complex.

---

# 23. He Changed How We Judge a No.6

Traditional questions for a defensive midfielder included:

**Can he tackle?**

**Can he cover?**

**Can he win duels?**

Busquets added new questions.

**Can he receive under pressure?**

**Can he manipulate the press?**

**Can he play through the first line?**

**Can he control tempo from deep?**

**Can he defend through positioning?**

```text
OLD No.6
 ↓
DEFEND SPACE


BUSQUETS No.6
 ↓
CONTROL SPACE
+
CONTROL PRESSURE
+
CONTROL POSSESSION
```

The technical standard of the position increased dramatically.

---

# 24. Makélélé vs Busquets — Different Types of Security

Both were elite No.6s.

But they solved danger differently.

### Makélélé

```text
ATTACK BREAKS DOWN
      ↓
PROTECT
      ↓
COVER
      ↓
STOP TRANSITION
```

Core idea:

**Security after Risk**

### Busquets

```text
BUILD-UP
   ↓
ATTRACT PRESS
   ↓
ESCAPE PRESS
   ↓
KEEP CONTROL
```

Core idea:

**Control before Risk**

Makélélé protected the team when freedom created danger.

Busquets tried to control the game so that danger appeared less often.

That is the key difference between EP02 and EP03.

---

# 25. The Real Busquets Revolution

Busquets introduced a different expectation for the No.6.

What if the defensive midfielder could:

attract the press,

create space,

control tempo,

decide the direction of build-up,

and protect the team through possession?

```text
DEFENSIVE MIDFIELDER
        ↓

PRESS CONTROLLER
        +
BUILD-UP HUB
        +
TEMPO CONTROLLER
        +
SPACE MANAGER
```

At that point, the No.6 became something more than a defensive specialist.

It became a control position.

---

# Conclusion — He Did Not Escape Pressure. He Controlled It.

Busquets was not great because he was faster than his opponents.

He was not great because he was stronger.

He simply thought earlier.

He invited pressure.

He waited.

He used the defender's movement.

A single touch could break a pressing line.

A short pause could move an entire midfield.

A simple pass could change the direction of the attack.

```text
ATTRACT
   ↓
WAIT
   ↓
RELEASE
   ↓
PROGRESS
   ↓
CONTROL
```

Makélélé showed the value of the No.6 through **Balance**.

Busquets pushed the position toward **Control**.

His defining skill was not tackling.

It was not dribbling.

It was not long passing.

It was:

**Understanding Pressure.**

Busquets did not avoid the press.

**He used it.**

And from that point on, the No.6 could no longer be judged only as a defensive midfielder.

He could also be the player who controlled the opponent's press and the rhythm of the entire match.

**Busquets did not beat the press with speed.
He made the press move where he wanted.**

Busquets and Casemiro played in similar areas of the pitch.
