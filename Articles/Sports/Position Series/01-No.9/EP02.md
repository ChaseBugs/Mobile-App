# EP02 — The Golden Age of No.9

## Why Did the 2000s Produce So Many Legendary Strikers?

### The Evolution of Football Positions — No.9

Look back at European football in the early 2000s and one thing immediately stands out:

**There were an extraordinary number of world-class strikers.**

Ronaldo Nazário.

Thierry Henry.

Andriy Shevchenko.

Christian Vieri.

Ruud van Nistelrooy.

Hernán Crespo.

Filippo Inzaghi.

David Trezeguet.

Raúl.

Fernando Morientes.

Michael Owen.

Roy Makaay.

Patrick Kluivert.

And shortly afterwards came players such as Didier Drogba, Samuel Eto'o and Zlatan Ibrahimović.

Today, having just one striker of that level can define an elite club's attack.

Back then, some major clubs had two or even three.

AC Milan had Shevchenko and Inzaghi.

Inter had Vieri and Crespo.

Real Madrid had Ronaldo, Raúl and Morientes across the period.

Juventus had Trezeguet and Del Piero.

The problem for elite clubs was sometimes not:

> **“Where can we find a great striker?”**

It was:

> **“How do we fit all these great forwards into the team?”**

So why did this happen?

Was it simply a freak generation in which many great goalscorers appeared at the same time?

Or did the football of that era create a better environment for producing and using elite No.9s?

The second explanation matters enormously.

## The early 2000s were built for strikers.

---

# 1. Football Needed More No.9s

Today, the 4-3-3 is one of football's most familiar structures.

```text
LW              CF              RW
```

There is usually only one central striker.

But in the late 1990s and early 2000s, two-striker formations were far more common.

The classic 4-4-2:

```text
          ST1          ST2

LM        CM            CM        RM

LB        CB            CB        RB
```

Or the 4-3-1-2:

```text
          ST1          ST2

                AM

          CM    CM    CM
```

The precise formation is not the important part.

The important point is simple:

## Teams needed two forwards.

Imagine 20 major clubs regularly using two strikers.

```text
20 Clubs × 2 Strikers
        =
40 Starting Striker Roles
```

Compare that with 20 teams using a lone centre-forward:

```text
20 Clubs × 1 CF
        =
20 Starting CF Roles
```

This is only an illustration, but it shows the structural difference.

There were simply **more high-level jobs available for specialist strikers**.

That influenced everything beneath the first team as well.

More opportunities meant more players could develop specifically as centre-forwards.

---

# 2. Strikers Did Not Have to Do Everything

Two-striker systems also allowed responsibilities to be divided.

```text
TARGET MAN          SECOND STRIKER

Strength            Movement
Heading             Creativity
Hold-up             Dribbling
Finishing           Link Play
```

Or even more simply:

```text
POACHER              CREATOR
```

One striker could battle the centre-backs.

The other could find space.

One could stay high.

The other could drop deeper.

One could concentrate on finishing.

The other could help create.

This meant a striker did not need to be complete in every area.

### If one or two qualities were exceptional, that could be enough.

And that created tremendous variety.

---

# 3. One Position, Many Different Masters

The great strikers of the era did not all play the same way.

| Player              | Archetype                  | Defining Quality                   |
| ------------------- | -------------------------- | ---------------------------------- |
| Ronaldo Nazário     | Complete / Explosive 9     | Dribbling, acceleration, finishing |
| Thierry Henry       | Mobile / Hybrid 9          | Speed, channels, creativity        |
| Christian Vieri     | Power 9                    | Strength, aerial threat, shooting  |
| Andriy Shevchenko   | Mobile Finisher            | Movement, pace, finishing          |
| Ruud van Nistelrooy | Box Predator               | Penalty-area movement, finishing   |
| Filippo Inzaghi     | Poacher                    | Timing, positioning                |
| Hernán Crespo       | Classic No.9               | Movement, heading, finishing       |
| David Trezeguet     | Penalty-Box 9              | One-touch finishing, movement      |
| Raúl                | Intelligent Second Forward | Movement, combinations, space      |
| Fernando Morientes  | Traditional CF             | Aerial play, penalty-box presence  |

This diversity is crucial to understanding the era.

Football did not demand:

> **“Every striker must have the same skill set.”**

Instead, coaches could build systems around what a particular forward did exceptionally well.

---

# 4. Inzaghi and Van Nistelrooy — The Art of the Penalty Area

Filippo Inzaghi is perhaps the clearest example of specialization.

His greatness was not based on spectacular dribbling or midfield play.

His real weapons were questions such as:

**Where will the ball fall?**

**Where is the defender looking?**

**When should I move?**

A poacher does not see only the ball.

He reads:

```text
BALL
 +
DEFENDER
 +
SPACE
 +
OFFSIDE LINE
```

When the defender turns his attention toward the ball, the striker moves into the blind side.

That half-second can decide a match.

Ruud van Nistelrooy represented another version of the penalty-box specialist.

His most dangerous world was not 40 metres from goal.

It was here:

```text
┌─────────────────────┐
│                     │
│       ● ST          │
│                     │
│        GOAL         │
└─────────────────────┘
```

Near post.

Far post.

Penalty spot.

Behind the centre-back.

In front of the centre-back.

Rebound zone.

The distances were small.

But small movements created goals.

In that era, mastering this space could make you a world-class player.

---

# 5. Vieri — A Striker Who Changed the Shape of the Team

Christian Vieri represented a completely different type of No.9.

```text
       LONG BALL
           ↓

        VIERI
       ↙     ↘
     CB       CB
```

He could fight two centre-backs.

Protect the ball.

Win headers.

Attack crosses.

Turn and shoot.

Push the defensive line backwards.

And this had a tactical effect far beyond his individual goals.

When a team was under pressure:

```text
Defence
   ↓
LONG BALL
   ↓
VIERI
   ↓
HOLD
   ↓
TEAM MOVES FORWARD
```

the striker became an escape route.

He gave midfielders and full-backs time to move up the pitch.

His presence itself changed where the game was played.

The No.9 could therefore be both a goalscorer and an **advancing platform for the entire team**.

---

# 6. Shevchenko, Henry and Ronaldo Were Already Pointing Toward the Future

Not every striker of the era was a static penalty-box specialist.

Some were already showing characteristics we now associate with modern forwards.

Andriy Shevchenko combined traditional finishing with mobility.

He could run behind.

Attack channels.

Counterattack.

Carry the ball.

And still finish inside the box.

Thierry Henry went even further.

Although nominally a centre-forward, he frequently moved toward the left channel.

```text
LEFT CHANNEL
     ↓
   HENRY
     ↓
DRIVE INSIDE
     ↓
  SHOOT
```

He could drag defenders away from central positions, accelerate into space, dribble inside and finish.

Those characteristics look remarkably familiar today.

**Wide Forward.**

**Inside Forward.**

**Hybrid No.9.**

Henry was not simply a great striker of the old era.

In many ways, he was previewing the forward of the future.

And then there was Ronaldo Nazário.

R9 was almost impossible to classify as a conventional striker.

```text
R9
 ↓
CB
 ↓
CB
 ↓
GK
 ↓
GOAL
```

He combined:

Acceleration.

Strength.

Balance.

Dribbling.

Technique.

Ball carrying.

And elite finishing.

He was simultaneously a No.9, a dribbler and a transition weapon.

Players with that combination would be extremely rare in any era.

---

# 7. The Entire Attack Was Designed to Feed the No.9

Perhaps the biggest difference between then and now is not the striker himself.

It is what everyone around him was trying to do.

A simplified early-2000s attack might look like this:

```text
FULL-BACK ─→ CROSS ─┐
                    │
WINGER ───→ CROSS ──┤
                    ▼
No.10 ─→ THROUGH → No.9 → GOAL
                    ▲
CM ───────→ PASS ───┤
                    │
CB ───→ LONG BALL ──┘
```

The winger attacked the full-back and crossed.

The No.10 looked for the decisive pass.

The midfielder supplied progression.

The centre-back could play directly toward the striker.

Different routes often had the same destination:

## The No.9.

The striker was not simply another attacker.

He was the **final reference point of the attacking system**.

---

# 8. Then the Goals Began to Move Away From the Centre

The relationship between winger and striker changed dramatically.

Traditionally:

```text
WINGER
   ↓
CROSS
   ↓
No.9
   ↓
GOAL
```

But the rise of goalscoring wide forwards changed the equation.

Cristiano Ronaldo.

Lionel Messi.

Arjen Robben.

And later many others.

The pattern increasingly became:

```text
WINGER
   ↓
CUT INSIDE
   ↓
SHOOT
   ↓
GOAL
```

Wide players stopped being only suppliers.

They became finishers.

Modern attacks could therefore distribute goals across several positions.

```text
          CF
       ↙  ↓  ↘
     LW ↔ AM ↔ RW
       ↖     ↗
        CM  CM
```

If the CF drops, the winger attacks the box.

If the winger comes inside, the CF can move away.

A midfielder can make the third-man run.

The attack becomes a network rather than a funnel toward one striker.

### The goalscorer did not disappear.

### The goals moved.

---

# 9. Direct Football Also Favoured the No.9

Another part of the old environment was a greater willingness to attack directly.

```text
CB → LONG PASS → ST
```

or:

```text
WINGER → CROSS → ST
```

or:

```text
SECOND BALL → ST
```

This naturally increased the importance of a strong central forward.

When short build-up was difficult, the team could bypass pressure by finding the striker.

If the No.9 won the duel or protected the ball, the entire team could advance.

This gave players with strength, aerial ability and hold-up play enormous tactical value.

---

# 10. Serie A: Great Strikers Against Great Defenders

The early-2000s Serie A provides perhaps the clearest example of the era's striker culture.

Vieri.

Shevchenko.

Inzaghi.

Crespo.

Trezeguet.

Del Piero.

Batistuta.

Montella.

Adriano.

At the same time, they were facing defenders such as:

Maldini.

Nesta.

Cannavaro.

Thuram.

Stam.

Samuel.

That environment demanded precision.

A striker could not rely only on power.

He had to develop:

Timing.

Body feints.

First touch.

Blind-side movement.

Positioning.

Finishing.

Awareness of the defensive line.

Strong defending did not eliminate elite strikers.

Paradoxically, it could force them to become even more specialized and intelligent.

---

# 11. Then the Ecosystem Changed

From the mid-2000s onward, several tactical developments began to reshape the position.

```text
4-4-2
  ↓
4-2-3-1
  ↓
4-3-3
```

But formation was only part of the story.

The wider transition looked more like this:

```text
Two Strikers
     ↓
One Striker

Classic Winger
     ↓
Inside Forward

Classic No.10
     ↓
No.8 / Hybrid Creator

Direct Football
     ↓
Positional Play

Passive Forward
     ↓
Pressing Forward

Specialist
     ↓
Multi-Functional Player
```

Three-man midfields became increasingly influential.

Possession football developed.

Pressing became more systematic.

Wide forwards became goalscorers.

The False 9 challenged the idea that a team even needed a conventional striker.

No single tactical innovation ended the golden age.

### The entire ecosystem changed.

---

# 12. The Real Meaning of the Golden Age

This is why describing the early 2000s as merely a “great generation of strikers” does not tell the whole story.

The environment itself was different.

```text
MORE STRIKER ROLES
        +
TWO-STRIKER SYSTEMS
        +
SPECIALIZATION
        +
CLASSIC WINGERS
        +
CREATIVE No.10s
        +
DIRECT FOOTBALL
        +
STRIKER-CENTRIC ATTACK
        =
THE GOLDEN AGE OF No.9
```

There were more positions for strikers.

Their roles could be specialized.

The attacking structure often served them directly.

And coaches could tolerate limitations if a striker possessed an exceptional match-winning weapon.

This allowed many different kinds of world-class No.9s to exist at the same time.

---

# Were the Old Strikers Simply Better?

Not necessarily.

That conclusion would be too easy.

Modern forwards are expected to perform far more tactical functions.

They press.

Rotate.

Link play.

Defend passing lanes.

Move into different zones.

Create space for teammates.

And still score.

But one thing is clear:

### The early 2000s offered a better environment for many different types of specialist striker to survive at the elite level.

Inzaghi could be Inzaghi.

Vieri could be Vieri.

Trezeguet could be Trezeguet.

Henry could be Henry.

They did not all have to pass the same tactical examination.

---

# 2000s vs Modern Football

Perhaps the difference can be summarized in two ideas.

### 2000s

```text
Find what the striker
does BEST.

        ↓

Build around it.
```

### Modern Football

```text
Find a striker

        ↓

who can do
EVERYTHING.
```

The old game was more willing to accommodate specialists.

Modern football increasingly demands complete players.

And that helps explain one of football's great paradoxes:

**The game has become more sophisticated, yet truly elite No.9s appear harder to find.**

---

# Conclusion — It Was Not Just a Great Generation. It Was a Great Era for Strikers.

We often look back at the early 2000s and say:

> **“Football simply had better strikers back then.”**

There is some truth in the nostalgia.

It was an extraordinary generation.

But there is a deeper explanation.

The football of that era gave strikers more opportunities to play, specialize and become stars.

Two-striker systems created more positions.

Creative No.10s supplied them.

Wingers delivered crosses.

Direct football made them reference points.

And coaches were more willing to accept a striker who was exceptional at a few decisive things rather than competent at everything.

That is why:

**Vieri could be the Power No.9.**

**Inzaghi could be the Poacher.**

**Van Nistelrooy could be the Box Predator.**

**Trezeguet could be the Penalty-Box Specialist.**

**Shevchenko could be the Mobile Finisher.**

**Henry could be the Hybrid Forward.**

**Ronaldo could simply be Ronaldo.**

They all played as forwards.

But they did not need to play the same way.

And that diversity may be the true beauty of the **Golden Age of the No.9**.
