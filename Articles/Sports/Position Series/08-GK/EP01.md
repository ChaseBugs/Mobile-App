# SERIES 08 — The Evolution of the Goalkeeper

# EP01 — When Did the Goalkeeper Become the 11th Outfield Player?

## The 20-Year Revolution of the Goalkeeper Position

31 May 2025, Munich.

Paris Saint-Germain beat Inter 5-0 to win the first UEFA Champions League title in the club's history.

At the centre of that triumph was their goalkeeper, **Gianluigi Donnarumma**.

In the round of 16 at Anfield, he saved two Liverpool penalties in the shootout.

In the quarter-final against Aston Villa and the semi-final against Arsenal, the decisive saves kept coming.

A goalkeeper who had just won Europe's biggest competition.

A goalkeeper who, at Euro 2020, became the first keeper ever named Player of the Tournament.

Yet barely two and a half months later, PSG left him out of their UEFA Super Cup squad.

In goal stood **Lucas Chevalier**, newly signed from Lille.

At the beginning of September, Donnarumma left for Manchester City.

Why would a Champions League-winning goalkeeper lose his place in the very same summer?

Luis Enrique's answer came down to one idea:

**"We want a different type of goalkeeper."**

That decision shows, in its most extreme form, what modern football now demands from a goalkeeper.

---

Traditionally, evaluating a goalkeeper was relatively simple.

**Can he stop shots?**

**Can he catch crosses?**

**Can he survive 1v1 situations?**

**Can he command his box?**

All of those questions still matter.

The first responsibility of a goalkeeper is still:

# STOP THE BALL

But modern football asks another question:

> **“What can you do with the ball when the opponent presses?”**

The modern goalkeeper is no longer only the last player who ends the opponent's attack.

**He is also the first player who starts his own team's attack.**

So when, and how, did this change begin?

---

# 1. It All Started With One Rule

The 1990 World Cup in Italy is remembered as one of the dullest tournaments in history.

It averaged just 2.21 goals per game — the lowest in World Cup history at the time.

Teams protecting a lead used the easiest time-wasting method available.

```text
DF → BACK PASS → GK
              PICKS IT UP
              ROLLS IT OUT
              DF → BACK PASS → GK
              PICKS IT UP AGAIN
```

The Republic of Ireland vs Egypt match at that tournament is still often cited as the classic example, with Ireland goalkeeper Packie Bonner keeping the ball in and around his hands for several minutes.

FIFA and IFAB decided to solve the problem.

From the 1992-93 season, straight after Euro 1992, the **back-pass rule** came into force.

A ball deliberately kicked to the goalkeeper by a teammate could no longer be handled.

That rule created a completely new requirement:

# THE GOALKEEPER MUST USE HIS FEET

At first, most goalkeepers simply kicked that ball as far away as possible.

But from that moment, the direction of the position had already started to change.

**A position defined by the hands began to become a position of the feet.**

---

# 2. The 2000s — The Age of the Shot-Stopper

Even after the back-pass rule, the core value of a goalkeeper remained in his hands for a long time.

Think of the great goalkeepers of the 2000s.

**Buffon**

**Casillas**

**Čech**

**Kahn**

At the 2006 World Cup, Buffon conceded only two goals in seven matches.

One was an own goal. The other was Zidane's penalty in the final.

No opponent scored past him from open play during the entire tournament.

Casillas led Spain to three consecutive major titles: Euro 2008, the 2010 World Cup and Euro 2012.

His save with his trailing foot against Robben in the 2010 World Cup final is still talked about today.

Their value lay mainly in:

**Reflexes**

**Positioning**

**Aerial command**

**1v1 defending**

**Concentration**

The ideal of the era could be summarised as:

# GUARD THE GOAL

The goalkeeper's territory was mainly this space:

```text
   ┌───────────────┐
   │   PENALTY     │
   │     BOX       │
   │   ┌───────┐   │
   │   │  GK   │   │
   └───┴───────┴───┘
```

That does not mean footwork was irrelevant.

Edwin van der Sar, an Ajax product, was excellent with his feet and took part in Manchester United's build-up.

But in that era, footwork was an **advantage**, not a **requirement**.

---

# 3. When the Defensive Line Moved Up, the Goalkeeper Had to Follow

From the late 2000s, teams increasingly used higher defensive lines.

Pressing sides such as Guardiola's Barcelona and Klopp's Dortmund needed to make the pitch small.

```text
   FW  FW  FW
     MF  MF
   DF  DF  DF  DF
───────────────── ← HIGH LINE

     EMPTY SPACE (30–40m)

         GK
```

But when the defensive line moves up, a huge space opens behind it.

Who protects that space?

The centre-backs alone are not enough.

So began the era in which the goalkeeper had to leave his box.

# HIGH LINE → SWEEPER KEEPER

The goalkeeper is no longer a player who only protects the goal.

He became the player who protects **the entire space behind the defensive line.**

---

# 4. 30 June 2014 — Neuer Shows a New Kind of Goalkeeper

2014 World Cup, round of 16. Germany vs Algeria in Porto Alegre.

Algeria repeatedly targeted the space behind Germany's high line.

Germany's defenders were troubled again and again by Algeria's fast counter-attacks.

And each time, the solution was not a defender.

It was the goalkeeper, **Manuel Neuer**.

```text
ALGERIA COUNTER-ATTACK
          ↓
RUN BEHIND GERMANY'S LINE
          ↓
NEUER ADVANCES UP TO 30m FROM GOAL
          ↓
TACKLE / CLEARANCE / HEADER
          ↓
DANGER REMOVED
```

Throughout the match, Neuer kept racing out of his box — sliding into tackles, heading the ball clear and passing it on to teammates.

Germany won 2-1 after extra time.

Watching that game, many people said the same thing:

**“The goalkeeper is playing like a libero.”**

Germany went on to win the tournament.

Neuer was the player who made Germany's high line possible.

Because of him, his defenders could defend further up the pitch.

# GOALKEEPER POSITION → TEAM POSITION

The goalkeeper's position had started to determine the position of the entire team.

*(Neuer's revolution is covered in detail in EP03.)*

---

# 5. Guardiola's Experiment — Hart, Bravo and Ederson

In the summer of 2016, Guardiola made a shocking decision almost as soon as he arrived at Manchester City.

He dropped **Joe Hart**, England's first-choice goalkeeper.

Instead, he signed **Claudio Bravo** from Barcelona.

Hart was loaned to Torino.

The message was clear:

> **“Our goalkeeper must take part in the build-up.”**

But the experiment did not succeed immediately.

Bravo's debut came in the Manchester derby at Old Trafford that September.

City won 2-1, but Bravo dropped a cross and Ibrahimović volleyed in.

As the season went on, the problem became clearer.

His footwork was good, but **too many of the shots he faced ended up in the net.**

City finished third in the league.

That failure taught an important lesson:

# FEET WITHOUT SAVES = NOT ENOUGH

In the summer of 2017, Guardiola signed **Ederson** from Benfica for around £35 million.

It was among the highest fees ever paid for a goalkeeper at the time.

Ederson turned Guardiola's idea into reality.

He could escape pressure with short passes.

He could also bypass an entire press with a single long ball.

And above all, **he could make saves.**

```text
OPPONENT PRESS ↑↑↑ (6–7 PLAYERS FORWARD)

GK ─────────────────→ FW
       ONE PASS
   70m PRECISION LONG BALL
```

One goalkeeper pass can bypass ten opponents.

In 2017-18, Ederson's first season, City won the league with 100 points — the first Premier League team ever to do so.

Over the following years, Ederson won six league titles and the 2023 treble with City, and even recorded Premier League assists.

# GOALKEEPER = FIRST PLAYMAKER

---

# 6. Liverpool's Choice — The Nightmare in Kyiv and Alisson

26 May 2018, Kyiv. Champions League final, Real Madrid vs Liverpool.

Liverpool goalkeeper **Loris Karius** made two fatal mistakes.

First, his throw hit Benzema, who was standing right in front of him, and rolled into the net.

Second, he fumbled a long-range shot from Bale that came straight at him.

Liverpool lost 3-1.

Tests at a hospital in the United States later revealed that Karius had been suffering from concussion symptoms after a collision with Ramos during the game.

But Liverpool's conclusion was clear:

**To win, the goalkeeper had to change.**

In July 2018, Liverpool signed **Alisson Becker** from Roma for around £66.8 million.

At the time, it was the highest fee ever paid for a goalkeeper.

(Only a month later, Chelsea broke that record by signing Kepa for around £71 million.)

Many people asked:

**“Is it right to spend that much on a goalkeeper?”**

Liverpool, led by their data analysis department, made a calculation.

A single goalkeeping error — or a single save — can change the points total of an entire season.

Alisson was special to them because he had both:

**World-class shot-stopping**

and

**The sweeping and footwork to support Klopp's high defensive line**

The impact was immediate.

```text
2017-18 LEAGUE GOALS CONCEDED: 38
              ↓
       ALISSON ARRIVES
              ↓
2018-19 LEAGUE GOALS CONCEDED: 22 (FEWEST IN THE LEAGUE)
```

In his first season, Alisson kept 21 clean sheets and won the Premier League Golden Glove.

Then came the most important moment of that season.

11 December 2018, Anfield. Final Champions League group game, Liverpool vs Napoli.

Liverpool led 1-0. One goal conceded, and they were out.

In stoppage time, Napoli's Milik found himself with a chance right in front of goal.

Alisson closed the angle quickly, stayed on his feet until the last moment and blocked the shot with his body.

After the game, Klopp said:

> **“If I'd known he was this good, I would have paid double.”**

That season, Liverpool beat Tottenham 2-0 in Madrid to win the Champions League.

One year after Kyiv.

The key lesson of Alisson's case is this:

# SAVES + FEET = COMPLETE GOALKEEPER

Liverpool did not simply buy a goalkeeper who was good with his feet.

They bought a goalkeeper who had **both shot-stopping and footwork.**

---

# 7. When the Goalkeeper Joins the Build-Up, a Numerical Advantage Appears

Why do coaches want goalkeepers who are good with their feet so badly?

The answer is in the numbers.

Imagine the opponent pressing with two strikers.

If the goalkeeper does not take part in the build-up:

```text
     ST     ST

  CB          CB

        (GK)
```

It is **2 vs 2**.

If the goalkeeper takes part:

```text
     ST     ST

  CB          CB

        GK
```

It becomes **3 vs 2**.

One player is always free.

That one spare player changes the entire build-up.

To solve this numerical disadvantage, the opponent must push another midfielder forward.

And then space opens in midfield.

# GK + 2 CB = NUMERICAL ADVANTAGE

That is why the modern goalkeeper is called **“the 11th outfield player.”**

---

# 8. The 2019 Goal-Kick Law Change

For the 2019-20 season, IFAB changed another important law.

Previously, a goal kick was only in play once the ball had left the penalty area.

Now, **teammates could receive the ball directly inside the box.**

```text
   ┌───────────────┐
   │  CB  GK  CB   │ ← SHORT BUILD-UP INSIDE THE BOX
   │      No.6     │
   └───────────────┘
```

With this change, the goal kick was no longer just a restart.

It became a:

# BUILD-UP START

Opponents began to press even higher.

And the goalkeeper had to face that pressure directly, throughout the match.

The goalkeeper now spends more time on the ball.

**And with that time comes more opportunity to make mistakes.**

---

# 9. The Day a Golden Glove Winner Left — De Gea and Onana

In 2022-23, Manchester United's **David de Gea** won the Premier League Golden Glove with 17 clean sheets.

Yet at the end of that season, United did not renew his contract.

After 12 years in United's goal, he left when his contract expired.

Erik ten Hag signed **André Onana** from Inter instead.

Onana had played under ten Hag at Ajax and had just helped Inter reach the 2023 Champions League final.

Above all, he was a goalkeeper **with excellent footwork.**

```text
DE GEA                    ONANA
SHOT-STOPPING ★★★★★      SHOT-STOPPING ★★★
FOOTWORK      ★★          FOOTWORK      ★★★★★
```

Onana's style had already caused controversy before.

At the 2022 World Cup in Qatar, Onana was dropped from Cameroon's squad and left mid-tournament.

According to reports, a disagreement with coach Rigobert Song over his style of play was one of the reasons.

His first season at United was not easy.

In the 2023-24 Champions League group stage, Onana's errors directly affected the results of important matches, and United finished bottom of their group.

That case leaves an important question:

**“How much can a goalkeeper's footwork replace his shot-stopping?”**

---

# 10. Arsenal's Choice — From Ramsdale to Raya

In the summer of 2023, Arsenal made a similar decision.

Aaron Ramsdale had been Arsenal's first-choice goalkeeper during their 2022-23 title challenge.

But Arteta brought in **David Raya** from Brentford.

The reason was build-up.

Raya could play calm short passes under pressure, and his long passing was highly accurate.

And in his first season, 2023-24, Raya won the Premier League Golden Glove.

He won it again in 2024-25.

Then came 2025-26.

Raya kept **19 clean sheets in 37 appearances** and won **his third consecutive Golden Glove.**

Arsenal won the Premier League with 85 points, seven clear of second-placed Manchester City.

**It was their first league title in 22 years — the first since the 2003-04 “Invincibles.”**

```text
2023  RAMSDALE → RAYA (A CHOICE MADE FOR BUILD-UP)
2024  GOLDEN GLOVE
2025  GOLDEN GLOVE
2026  GOLDEN GLOVE + PREMIER LEAGUE TITLE
```

Arsenal's choice was proven right by the results.

**De Gea → Onana** and **Ramsdale → Raya**.

Both clubs changed goalkeeper for the same reason.

But the results were different.

What was the difference?

# SYSTEM FIT

Changing goalkeeper is not simply a matter of signing “a player who is good with his feet.”

It is about finding a goalkeeper who **fits the team's structure, defensive line and build-up method.**

---

# 11. Back to PSG — Why Let Donnarumma Go?

Now let us return to the original question.

Donnarumma led PSG to a treble in 2024-25.

In the Champions League knockout rounds in particular, he saved PSG again and again.

```text
ROUND OF 16  vs Liverpool   → 2 SHOOTOUT SAVES
QUARTER-FINAL vs Aston Villa → DECISIVE SAVES
SEMI-FINAL   vs Arsenal     → DECISIVE SAVES
FINAL        vs Inter       → 5-0 CHAMPIONS
```

So why did PSG let him go?

The first reason was his **contract**.

It was approaching its final year, and renewal talks had stalled over salary.

But the more important reason was **style**.

Luis Enrique's PSG wanted an elaborate build-up that begins with the goalkeeper.

Donnarumma was a world-class shot-stopper.

But footwork and passing decisions under pressure were not his greatest strengths.

PSG signed **Lucas Chevalier** from Lille.

At Lille, Chevalier had been a goalkeeper who took an active part in short build-up, even under pressure.

Then came the UEFA Super Cup against Tottenham in Udine in August 2025.

Donnarumma was not in the squad.

On social media, he said he had been told he would no longer be part of the group.

```text
PSG'S JUDGEMENT

GREAT SHOT-STOPPER
        ≠
THE GOALKEEPER OUR SYSTEM NEEDS
```

There is an interesting twist to this decision.

The club that signed Donnarumma was Manchester City.

The very club where Guardiola had started the ball-playing goalkeeper revolution.

And at the same time, City sold Ederson to Fenerbahçe.

```text
PSG   : Donnarumma OUT → Chevalier (FOOTWORK)
CITY  : Ederson OUT    → Donnarumma (SHOT-STOPPING)
```

In the same summer, two clubs made choices in completely opposite directions.

But the story did not end there.

Chevalier's first season was not easy.

His build-up play matched what PSG wanted.

But he looked vulnerable on crosses and set pieces, and some of his mistakes led directly to goals.

In January 2026, for a Champions League game against Newcastle, Luis Enrique left Chevalier out and started **Matvey Safonov**.

Only a month earlier, Safonov had produced a series of shootout saves in the Intercontinental Cup.

By the second half of the season, Safonov was PSG's first-choice goalkeeper.

30 May 2026, Budapest. Champions League final, PSG vs Arsenal.

It was 1-1 after extra time, and PSG won the shootout 4-3.

PSG had won back-to-back Champions League titles.

**The man in goal was neither Donnarumma nor Chevalier. It was Safonov.**

At the other end, Raya saved Nuno Mendes' penalty, but it was not enough to win the trophy.

Meanwhile at Manchester City, Donnarumma kept 15 league clean sheets and finished second in the Golden Glove race.

```text
SUMMER 2025  PSG: Donnarumma OUT → Chevalier IN (FOOTWORK)
JAN 2026     PSG: Chevalier BENCHED → Safonov STARTS
MAY 2026     PSG: CL CHAMPIONS AGAIN WITH SAFONOV
```

That single year captures the heart of the modern goalkeeper debate.

**The system wanted a goalkeeper who was good with his feet.**

**But in the end, the team was protected by a goalkeeper who stopped goals.**

# THE SYSTEM CHOOSES — BUT THE SAVES DECIDE

**There is no single definition of a good goalkeeper.**

*(This debate is explored fully in EP05, “Shot-Stopper vs Ball-Playing Goalkeeper.”)*

---

# 12. The Press Targets the Goalkeeper

Modern pressing teams look for the weak link.

A goalkeeper who is uncomfortable on the ball becomes the target.

```text
BLOCK THE CB PASS
        ↓
FORCE THE BALL TO THE GK
        ↓
STRIKER PRESSES
        ↓
LONG BALL OR MISTAKE
```

If the goalkeeper cannot pass under pressure,

the team is eventually forced to go long.

And a large share of those long balls go straight back to the opponent.

# WEAK FEET = LOST POSSESSION

In modern football, a goalkeeper's footwork is connected to the team's entire possession structure.

---

# 13. But a Goalkeeper's Mistake Is the Most Expensive

Here lies a problem unique to the goalkeeper position.

When a winger loses the ball, several defenders are still behind him.

When a centre-back loses the ball, the goalkeeper is still there.

But when the goalkeeper loses the ball:

```text
GK MISTAKE
    ↓
EMPTY GOAL
    ↓
GOAL CONCEDED
```

There is nobody behind him.

That is what happened to Karius in Kyiv, and to Bravo at Old Trafford.

So what the modern goalkeeper needs is not unconditional boldness.

It is:

# CONTROLLED RISK

Knowing when to play short,

when to go long,

and when simply to clear the ball safely.

**Judgement matters more than passing technique.**

---

# 14. A Goalkeeper Is Still a Goalkeeper

The phrase “the 11th outfield player” can be misleading.

The evolution of the goalkeeper is not:

**Goalkeeper → Outfield Player**

It is:

# GOALKEEPER + OUTFIELD PLAYER

What the modern goalkeeper still needs:

Shot-stopping.

Positioning.

Dealing with crosses.

1v1 defending.

Commanding the box.

What has been added:

Passing.

Scanning.

Composure.

Sweeping.

Reading the press.

Modern football did not remove the goalkeeper's traditional responsibilities.

**It simply added new ones on top of them.**

Alisson's save against Napoli. Donnarumma's shootout at Anfield.

In the end, what most directly decides the fate of a season is still **the decisive save.**

---

# 15. How to Watch a Modern Goalkeeper

Instead of only counting saves, watch four things.

### 1. Starting Position

Where does the goalkeeper stand when the opponent attacks? Is the distance between him and his defensive line right?

### 2. Sweeping

How quickly does he decide and come out when the ball is played behind the defence?

### 3. Distribution Choice

Under pressure, does he choose the short pass, the long pass or the clearance?

### 4. Communication

Is he organising his defensive line and giving information to his teammates?

These four qualities connect to one concept:

# DECISION QUALITY

---

# 16. From Last Defender to First Attacker

The goalkeeper is the last player protecting his own goal.

But the moment he wins the ball, his role changes.

```text
OPPONENT ATTACK
      ↓
GK SAVES / SWEEPS
      ↓
BALL WON
      ↓
GK BUILDS
      ↓
PRESS BROKEN
      ↓
OUR ATTACK
```

He ends one phase and begins the next.

That is the biggest change to happen to the goalkeeper position over the last 30 years.

```text
1992   BACK-PASS RULE        → THE GOALKEEPER MUST USE HIS FEET
2000s  BUFFON · CASILLAS     → PEAK OF THE SHOT-STOPPER
2014   NEUER                 → SWEEPER KEEPER
2017   EDERSON               → FIRST PLAYMAKER
2018   ALISSON               → COMPLETE GOALKEEPER
2019   GOAL-KICK LAW CHANGE  → THE START OF BUILD-UP
2025   DONNARUMMA LEAVES PSG → THE SYSTEM CHOOSES THE GOALKEEPER
2026   SAFONOV · RAYA        → SAVES AND BUILD-UP, TWO PATHS SIDE BY SIDE
```

# SAVE → SWEEP → BUILD → START

---

# Conclusion — Protect With the Hands, Start With the Feet

A goalkeeper who cannot make saves cannot be an elite goalkeeper.

Bravo's failure showed that.

But it is equally clear that saves alone are no longer enough.

Even a Champions League-winning goalkeeper can lose his place if he does not fit the system.

Donnarumma's departure showed that.

And the fact that PSG still won the Champions League with Safonov showed that, however important the system becomes, **shot-stopping remains the foundation.**

What has changed is **what happens after the save.**

Can he read the press?

Can he find the free teammate?

Can he protect the space behind the defensive line?

Does he know when not to take a risk?

The modern goalkeeper now performs two roles at the same time:

**the last barrier against the opponent's attack**

and

**the first starting point of his own team's attack.**

> **He protects the goal with his hands — and starts the attack with his feet.**
