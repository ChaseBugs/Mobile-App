# Why Are Elite No.9s So Rare Today?

## The Decline of the Traditional Centre-Forward and the Golden Age of the 2000s

Football fans who remember the early 2000s often notice something strange about the modern game:

**Why are truly elite centre-forwards so rare today?**

Between roughly 2000 and 2006, almost every major European club seemed to have a famous No.9.

Inter had **Christian Vieri** and Hernán Crespo.

AC Milan had **Andriy Shevchenko** and Filippo Inzaghi.

Arsenal had **Thierry Henry**.

Real Madrid had **Ronaldo Nazário, Raúl, and Fernando Morientes**.

Juventus had **David Trezeguet**.

Manchester United had **Ruud van Nistelrooy**.

Chelsea had Crespo and later Didier Drogba.

Bayern had Roy Makaay.

And it was not unusual for one elite club to have two or even three top-class strikers at the same time.

Today, the situation feels very different.

Barcelona paid a significant fee for Robert Lewandowski even though he was already 33 when they signed him.

Arsenal spent years searching for a decisive central goalscorer before eventually signing Viktor Gyökeres.

Real Madrid have world-class attackers, but their system has often relied on mobile forwards rather than being built around a classic penalty-box No.9 in the traditional sense.

So what happened?

Did the world simply stop producing great strikers?

Or did football itself stop developing them?

The answer is more complicated.

**The traditional No.9 did not disappear.**

Instead, the position changed so dramatically that becoming an elite centre-forward became much more difficult.

---

# 1. First, What Exactly Is a Traditional No.9?

The traditional centre-forward had a simple primary responsibility:

**Score goals.**

Especially inside the penalty area.

A classic No.9 typically operated like this:

```text
                CF
                ●
           CB       CB

LW                          RW
```

He played between the centre-backs.

He attacked crosses.

He made runs behind the defensive line.

He fought for second balls.

He held off defenders.

He attacked the near post.

He attacked the far post.

And when a chance appeared, he finished it.

For a traditional No.9, the most important question was not:

**“How involved is he in build-up?”**

It was:

**“Can he turn one chance into one goal?”**

That made the striker role far more specialized.

---

# 2. Why Were There So Many No.9s in the Early 2000s?

The first major reason is tactical structure.

In the early 2000s, two-striker systems were much more common.

The classic example was the 4-4-2:

```text
          ST1       ST2

LM        CM        CM        RM

LB        CB        CB        RB
```

Another popular system was 4-3-1-2:

```text
          ST       ST

              AM

        CM    CM    CM
```

These formations required **two starting strikers**.

That matters enormously.

Imagine 20 major clubs all using two forwards.

You need:

**20 × 2 = 40 starting strikers**

Now imagine those same clubs playing modern 4-3-3 systems:

```text
LW          CF          RW
```

Now you only need:

**20 × 1 = 20 centre-forwards**

The number of available striker positions effectively falls dramatically.

That changes the entire talent-development ecosystem.

---

# 3. Two-Striker Systems Allowed Specialists to Survive

Playing with two forwards also allowed the responsibilities to be divided.

One striker could be:

**the target man**

while the other was:

**the mobile second striker**

Or one could be:

**the finisher**

while the other was:

**the creator**

For example:

```text
     FINISHER         CREATOR
```

One player could stay close to goal.

The other could drop deeper.

This meant that a pure goalscorer did not need to do everything.

A player like Inzaghi did not have to become an elite dribbler.

He did not need to play 50-metre diagonal passes.

He did not have to drop into midfield and organize possession.

His greatest responsibility was understanding:

**Where should I be when the chance arrives?**

That kind of specialization was perfectly acceptable.

---

# 4. The Modern Lone Striker Has Too Many Responsibilities

Most modern elite teams use formations based around:

**4-3-3**

or

**4-2-3-1**

That means there is usually only one central striker.

```text
LW          CF          RW

             AM
```

Because there is only one CF, the coach expects that player to perform many different functions.

The modern striker must:

* score goals,
* press centre-backs,
* block passing lanes,
* drop into midfield,
* link with wingers,
* hold the ball,
* run behind the defence,
* create space for inside forwards,
* attack crosses,
* defend from the front,
* lead counter-pressing,
* make channel runs,
* occupy both centre-backs.

In other words, the ideal modern striker is almost expected to combine:

**Vieri's strength**

*

**Inzaghi's movement**

*

**Henry's speed**

*

**Bergkamp's link play**

*

**Tevez's pressing**

*

**Ronaldo Nazário's finishing**

That player is extremely rare.

This is one of the biggest reasons elite No.9s are now so expensive.

---

# 5. The False 9 Revolution

One of the biggest tactical changes affecting the traditional striker was the rise of the **False 9**.

Pep Guardiola's Barcelona provided the most famous example through Lionel Messi.

A traditional No.9 stays high:

```text
        CB     CF     CB
```

A False 9 drops deeper:

```text
        CB          CB

              ↓
             Messi

          CM      CM
```

This creates a dilemma for the centre-backs.

If they follow him:

```text
CB → Messi

          SPACE
            ↑
         Winger
```

space opens behind them.

If they do not follow:

```text
        Messi FREE
```

Messi receives freely between the lines.

This tactical idea was so successful that many coaches began asking:

**Do we even need a traditional striker?**

For several years, the football world became increasingly interested in fluid front lines instead of fixed No.9s.

---

# 6. Messi and Cristiano Ronaldo Changed Where Goals Came From

Another major change came from players such as Lionel Messi and Cristiano Ronaldo.

They demonstrated that your primary goalscorer did not have to be a traditional centre-forward.

The old model was:

```text
Winger → CF → GOAL
```

The modern model became:

```text
          CF
        ↙    ↘

     LW        RW
      ↓        ↓

    GOALS    GOALS
```

Wide forwards became elite scorers.

Cristiano Ronaldo.

Lionel Messi.

Mohamed Salah.

Sadio Mané.

Son Heung-min.

Vinícius Júnior.

As wingers and inside forwards became more productive, teams could distribute scoring responsibility across the front line.

That reduced the need for every team to build its attack around a specialist No.9.

---

# 7. Goals Became More Distributed

The classic attacking structure often looked something like this:

```text
           No.9
        25–30 goals

      Second Striker
         10–15 goals

        Midfield
         5–10 goals
```

Modern systems can look more like:

```text
LW         CF         RW

20         15         20

           AM
           10
```

The centre-forward may still score heavily, but he is no longer necessarily the only major scorer.

This has changed youth development as well.

If goals can come from wide players, attacking midfielders, and even full-backs, clubs do not always feel pressure to develop a pure penalty-box specialist from a young age.

---

# 8. Youth Development Changed

This may be one of the most important explanations.

In the past, if a young player looked like a natural striker, coaches often developed him specifically as a striker.

Training focused on:

**Box Movement**

**Headers**

**One-Touch Finishing**

**Near-Post Runs**

**Far-Post Runs**

**Rebounds**

**Centre-Back Duels**

**Offside-Line Timing**

**First-Time Shooting**

The objective was clear:

**produce a goalscorer.**

Modern academies increasingly emphasize:

**Technique**

**Possession**

**Pressing**

**Versatility**

**Position Rotation**

**Combination Play**

**Tactical Intelligence**

These are valuable developments.

They create technically complete footballers.

But there is a possible side effect.

We may produce more:

* attacking midfielders,
* hybrid wingers,
* No.8s,
* inside forwards,
* false 9s,

while producing fewer pure penalty-box specialists.

---

# 9. Goalscoring Instinct Is Difficult to Manufacture

One of the reasons elite No.9s are rare is that many of their most important skills are extremely subtle.

Consider this situation:

```text
        CB        CB
            CF
```

An average striker may simply stand between the centre-backs.

An elite striker moves into the defender's blind side:

```text
        CB       CB
                    CF
```

Then, as the cross is delivered:

```text
        CB      CB
                 ↘
                  CF → Near Post
```

He moves half a second earlier.

That half-second creates the goal.

This cannot be explained only by a "Finishing 90" attribute.

Elite No.9s need:

**Timing**

**Anticipation**

**Defender Manipulation**

**Body Orientation**

**Rebound Prediction**

**Composure**

**Spatial Awareness**

These qualities are developed over years of repetition.

They are not easily produced by general technical training.

---

# 10. In the Past, a Striker Could Have a Quiet Game and Still Succeed

This is another important difference.

Imagine Inzaghi touches the ball only 15 times.

He does almost nothing for 80 minutes.

Then he scores.

In the traditional evaluation of the striker role:

**Mission accomplished.**

Modern coaches may be less satisfied.

If a CF spends 70 minutes:

not pressing,

not linking play,

not creating space,

not helping defensively,

not offering a build-up outlet,

then even one goal may not completely excuse the rest of his performance.

The pure **poacher** therefore has fewer environments in which he can survive.

---

# 11. Pressing Completely Changed the Striker Position

In modern football, the striker is also the first defender.

Imagine the opposition building from the back:

```text
          CB1       CB2

               DM
```

The striker cannot simply stand and wait.

He may need to press like this:

```text
              CF
             ↙

          CB1       CB2
```

His pressing angle can simultaneously pressure the centre-back and block access to the defensive midfielder.

The striker's body orientation may determine the pressing direction of the entire team.

```text
             CF ↘

       CB1        CB2
                     ↓
                    TRAP
```

This means modern No.9s need defensive tactical intelligence as well as finishing ability.

That was not always demanded to the same extent from classic poachers.

---

# 12. Why Players Like Haaland Are So Valuable

Erling Haaland demonstrates why elite modern No.9s are incredibly valuable.

He combines several rare attributes:

**Elite physical power**

*

**Elite movement behind the line**

*

**Elite penalty-box movement**

*

**Elite finishing**

*

**Elite acceleration into space**

A player with this combination is extremely difficult to produce.

Economically, the problem is simple:

```text
Supply ↓↓↓

Demand ↑↑↑

Value ↑↑↑
```

Almost every major club wants a great No.9.

Very few truly elite ones exist.

That scarcity dramatically increases their market value.

---

# 13. Why Barcelona Signed Lewandowski

Barcelona's decision to sign Robert Lewandowski illustrates the problem perfectly.

He was already 33.

Yet Barcelona were still willing to invest significantly in him.

Why?

Because they were not simply buying a famous player.

They were buying a **fully developed No.9 skill package**.

Lewandowski offered:

* elite box movement,
* one-touch finishing,
* two-footed shooting,
* heading,
* centre-back manipulation,
* hold-up play,
* combination play,
* penalty-box intelligence,
* experience.

Developing all those qualities in one player can take many years.

Barcelona could instead buy a finished product.

That is the value of a true No.9.

---

# 14. Arsenal and Gyökeres — The Same Problem in a Different Form

Arsenal became another interesting example.

They already had:

good possession,

strong pressing,

creative midfielders,

dangerous wingers,

excellent defensive organization,

strong set pieces.

But eventually every attacking system faces one final question:

```text
Who finishes?
```

You can create a beautiful sequence:

```text
Build-Up
   ↓
Midfield Control
   ↓
Wing Combination
   ↓
Chance Creation
   ↓
?????
   ↓
GOAL
```

That final question mark matters enormously.

A player who can consistently convert chances changes the entire team.

This helps explain why clubs that appear tactically complete still search aggressively for elite centre-forwards.

---

# 15. Real Madrid Are a Different Type of Example

Real Madrid show another way modern teams can solve the No.9 problem.

They can use elite attackers who are not purely traditional strikers.

A player such as Kylian Mbappé can operate centrally and score enormous numbers of goals.

But stylistically, his greatest strength is often attacking space:

```text
SPACE
  ↓

Mbappé → → → GOAL
```

That is different from the traditional striker who constantly occupies both centre-backs:

```text
        CB   No.9   CB
```

So instead of organizing the entire attack around a classic penalty-box reference point, a modern team can distribute centre-forward responsibilities among several world-class attackers.

That is increasingly common.

---

# 16. Compare That With Early-2000s Milan

AC Milan provide a perfect contrast.

They could have:

**Andriy Shevchenko**

and

**Filippo Inzaghi**

available in the same squad.

The tactical question was not:

**“Where can we find one elite striker?”**

It was:

**“How do we fit multiple elite strikers into the same team?”**

That difference is striking.

Inter could similarly possess players such as:

**Christian Vieri**

**Hernán Crespo**

**Álvaro Recoba**

in overlapping periods.

The supply of high-level specialist forwards appeared much deeper.

---

# 17. Early-2000s Football Also Favoured Strikers More

The style of attacking football was also different.

There was relatively greater emphasis on:

**Crosses**

**Long Balls**

**Second Balls**

**Direct Passing**

A simple attacking sequence could be:

```text
RB → CROSS → CF
```

Or:

```text
CB → LONG BALL → CF → Second Striker
```

This environment naturally increased the value of players such as:

Vieri,

Trezeguet,

Van Nistelrooy,

Crespo,

Inzaghi.

The striker was frequently the final destination of the attack.

---

# 18. Modern Central Areas Are Extremely Crowded

Modern defensive organization has become highly sophisticated.

Teams compress central space.

```text
        ● ● ● ●
        ● ● ● ●

           CF
```

The striker may be surrounded by multiple defenders and midfielders.

So he cannot simply wait between the centre-backs.

He has to move.

```text
        ● ● ● ●

          CF ↓

             CM
```

He drops deeper.

Moves into channels.

Links possession.

Drags defenders.

Rotates positions.

Modern centre-forwards increasingly have to **think like midfielders while finishing like strikers**.

That is a very difficult combination.

---

# 19. Modern Defenders Are Also Better Prepared

It is important not to romanticize the past.

The early 2000s had extraordinary defenders:

Nesta.

Maldini.

Cannavaro.

Thuram.

Ferdinand.

Stam.

Nesta and Maldini in particular were among the greatest defenders ever.

The difference is not that modern defenders are individually superior to every defender from the past.

The bigger difference is the sophistication of the **collective defensive system**.

Modern teams use:

**Video Analysis**

**GPS Data**

**Compact Blocks**

**Pressing Triggers**

**Rest Defence**

**Detailed Opponent Analysis**

The striker is not just fighting one centre-back.

He is fighting an entire defensive mechanism.

---

# 20. The Change Was Already Visible by the Mid-2000s

This evolution did not suddenly appear in the 2010s.

The transition was already underway by the middle of the 2000s.

Football increasingly shifted toward three-man midfields.

Coaches wanted greater control in central areas.

More midfielders meant:

less space,

more passing options,

more tactical control.

Gradually, the game moved from:

```text
1990s / Early 2000s
STRIKER-CENTRIC
        ↓
Mid-2000s
MIDFIELD CONTROL
        ↓
2010s
POSSESSION + PRESSING
        ↓
2020s
POSITIONAL + TRANSITION FOOTBALL
```

The striker became only one part of a much larger tactical structure.

---

# 21. The Decline of the No.10 and the Transformation of the No.9 Are Connected

There is an important parallel here.

Football once had many classic No.10s:

Rui Costa.

Riquelme.

Aimar.

Totti.

Zidane.

These players were often given freedom to create.

Modern coaches increasingly demand more from attacking midfielders.

They must:

press,

track runners,

cover space,

rotate positions,

run without the ball.

As a result, the specialist No.10 became less common.

Exactly the same thing happened to the specialist No.9.

Modern football increasingly prefers:

**multi-functional players**

over

**single-function specialists.**

---

# 22. What Does the Modern Coach Want From a No.9?

The ideal modern striker changes roles depending on the phase of play.

## During Possession

```text
CB → CM → CF ↓
             ↓
          Link Play
```

He drops and connects.

## During High Press

```text
       CF → CB
```

He leads the press.

## During Transition

```text
CF → → → SPACE
```

He threatens depth.

## Inside the Box

```text
Cross → CF → GOAL
```

He becomes a classic finisher.

## Against a Deep Block

```text
CB
 ↓
CF holds ball
 ↓
Team advances
```

He may need to play as a target man.

Therefore, the modern CF may have to become:

**Poacher**

**Target Man**

**False 9**

**Pressing Forward**

**Channel Runner**

**Link Player**

all in the same match.

This explains the scarcity.

---

---

# 24. But We Should Not Simply Say “Old Strikers Were Better”

There is also a danger of nostalgia.

The early 2000s clearly had an extraordinary generation of centre-forwards.

But the tactical environment also made those players more visible.

Modern football still produces extraordinary goalscorers.

The difference is that many of them would historically have been classified as:

**second strikers**

or

**wide forwards**

rather than classic No.9s.

The location of goals has changed.

Past:

```text
          ★ CF ★
```

Modern:

```text
★ LW      CF      RW ★
```

So the goalscorer did not disappear.

**The goals moved.**

---

# 25. And Now the No.9 May Be Coming Back

Football moves in cycles.

False 9 systems and highly fluid attacks became increasingly popular.

Defences responded.

They became:

more compact,

better at blocking central combinations,

better at protecting spaces between the lines.

As a result, a powerful striker who can physically occupy centre-backs has become valuable again.

The tactical cycle can be simplified like this:

```text
Traditional No.9
       ↓
False 9 Revolution
       ↓
Fluid Front Three
       ↓
Defences Adapt
       ↓
Need a Real No.9 Again
```

This is why specialist goalscorers may become increasingly valuable again.

---

# 26. Gyökeres Represents the Return of the Specialist Finisher

This is what makes the Arsenal situation so interesting.

A sophisticated team can already have:

elite possession,

elite pressing,

elite midfield control,

strong wide players,

strong defence.

But ultimately football still contains one brutal question:

**Who puts the ball in the net?**

No tactical system completely eliminates that problem.

A true striker can provide:

```text
1 chance
   ↓
1 shot
   ↓
1 goal
```

That efficiency changes knockout matches, title races, and difficult games against deep defensive blocks.

So even after years of tactical evolution, football can eventually return to the classic No.9.

# 28. How Should We Train the Next Generation of No.9s?

If football wants to produce more elite centre-forwards, striker-specific development must return.

But it should not simply copy old-school training.

The ideal approach combines two worlds.

## PART A — OLD-SCHOOL STRIKER TRAINING

Develop:

**Box Movement**

**Near-Post Runs**

**Far-Post Runs**

**Blind-Side Movement**

**Rebounds**

**One-Touch Finishing**

**Headers**

**Centre-Back Manipulation**

**Offside-Line Timing**

These are specialist No.9 skills.

---

## PART B — MODERN STRIKER TRAINING

Also develop:

**Pressing**

**Link Play**

**First Touch Under Pressure**

**Dropping Between Lines**

**Channel Running**

**Counter-Pressing**

**Combination Play**

**Tactical Rotation**

The striker of the future must combine both.

The ideal formula is:

**Old-School Killer + Modern Team Player**

---

# 29. The Real Reason No.9s Feel So Rare

There are several reasons working together.

The decline of two-striker formations reduced the number of CF positions.

The rise of 4-3-3 moved goals toward wide forwards.

The False 9 reduced dependence on traditional centre-forwards.

Pressing increased defensive responsibilities.

Possession football increased link-play responsibilities.

Youth academies prioritized versatility.

Defensive systems became more compact.

And clubs raised the technical expectations for the position.

The result is a paradox.

Football players are generally becoming more technically complete.

But the truly elite specialist striker appears to be becoming **rarer**.

---

# Conclusion — The No.9 Did Not Die. Becoming a No.9 Became Much Harder.

The early 2000s represented a remarkable period for centre-forwards.

Vieri.

Ronaldo.

Henry.

Crespo.

Inzaghi.

Shevchenko.

Raúl.

Morientes.

Trezeguet.

Van Nistelrooy.

There were world-class strikers everywhere.

That was not purely coincidence.

The football environment helped create them.

Teams frequently used two strikers.

The striker role was more specialized.

Goals were concentrated centrally.

Youth systems deliberately trained penalty-box specialists.

Modern football is different.

Teams usually play one striker.

Goals are distributed toward the wings.

Centre-forwards must press.

They must link play.

They must rotate positions.

They must defend from the front.

They must run behind.

They must hold the ball.

And after doing all of that, they still have to score.

The old question for a striker was:

**“Can you score?”**

The modern questions are:

**Can you score?**

**Can you press?**

**Can you link play?**

**Can you run behind?**

**Can you hold the ball?**

**Can you create space for others?**

**Can you defend from the front?**

**Can you play against a low block?**

**Can you survive against a high press?**

That is why a true world-class No.9 is now so valuable.

The position did not disappear.

Its job description expanded.

And because the job became harder, the players capable of performing it at the highest level became rarer.

**The No.9 is not dead.**

In fact, the opposite may be happening.

**Because the true No.9 has become so rare, his value is rising again.**
