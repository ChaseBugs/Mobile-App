# EP04 — From No.10 to No.8

## Why Did the Playmaker Move Into Central Midfield?

For decades, the playmaker had an obvious home.

Behind the striker.

Between midfield and defence.

The classic No.10 zone.

But modern football increasingly places creative players deeper.

Modrić.

Kroos.

Ødegaard.

Bellingham.

They are not the same type of player, but they share one important function:

**They decide where the attack goes next.**

So why did so much playmaking move from No.10 to No.8?

The basic idea is simple.

**To gain more space, more vision, and more influence over the whole attack.**

---

# 1. Higher Up Means Less Time

The traditional No.10 usually receives high up the pitch.

```text
        ST

       No.10

   CM        CM
```

The advantage is obvious.

He is closer to goal.

But there is a problem.

When receiving between the lines, pressure can come from both directions.

```text
        CB
         ↓
       No.10
         ↑
         DM
```

A defender is in front.

A midfielder is behind.

The No.10 may have only a second to control, turn and create.

A No.8 can begin from a deeper position.

```text
        ST

   LW        RW

      No.8

       DM
```

Here, he can often receive facing forward.

That changes everything.

He has more time to scan the pitch and more space to decide what happens next.

---

# 2. From a Deeper Position, You Can See More of the Game

A high No.10 often sees the final attacking picture.

```text
       ST

      No.10
     ↙     ↘
   LW       RW
```

The No.8 can see a much larger picture.

```text
        ST
    LW      RW

       No.8
    ↙   ↓   ↘

  LB   DM    RB
```

He can see the forwards.

The other midfielders.

The full-backs.

And often the opposite side of the pitch.

This changes the meaning of playmaking.

The No.8 does not only create the **final pass**.

He can decide:

Where should the attack begin?

Which side should we attack?

Should we accelerate?

Should we keep possession?

Should we switch the play?

The playmaker becomes less of a final-action specialist and more of an **attack director**.

---

# 3. Modrić — Playmaking Starts Before Receiving the Ball

Luka Modrić is a perfect example of the modern creative No.8.

One of his greatest qualities is not simply what he does after receiving the ball.

It is how he prepares **before receiving it**.

```text
Ball → MODRIĆ
          ↗
    Body already open
```

He scans.

Adjusts his position.

Opens his body.

Then receives.

Because of that preparation, one touch can immediately lead into the next action.

Turn forward.

Escape pressure.

Switch direction.

Find a teammate between the lines.

### Modrić's pattern:

**Scan → Receive → Turn → Progress**

The classic No.10 often created the final attacking picture.

Modrić could help create the conditions that allowed that picture to exist in the first place.

---

# 4. Kroos — The Playmaker Does Not Need to Be Close to Goal

Toni Kroos pushed this idea even further.

He often controlled matches from much deeper positions.

Why?

Because distance from goal can also create perspective.

```text
           ST

LW                     RW

         KROOS
      ↙    ↓    ↘

    LB    DM     RB
```

From there, Kroos could play short.

Break a line with a forward pass.

Or switch the entire attack to the opposite side.

His job was not always to make the assist.

Sometimes his most important contribution happened several passes before the goal.

This is an important evolution of playmaking.

**Creativity is not only about creating the final chance.**

It can also mean creating the conditions for the chance to appear.

---

# 5. The No.8's Key Territory — The Half-Space

To understand modern midfield play, we need one important concept:

**The Half-Space.**

It is the channel between the centre and the wing.

```text
| Wide | Half | Centre | Half | Wide |
```

Why is this area so valuable?

In the centre, pressure can arrive from many directions.

Near the touchline, the player's options become limited.

The half-space offers a useful balance.

```text
           ST
            ↑
          No.8
         ↙    ↘
       LW      RW
```

From here, a midfielder can:

pass centrally,

combine with the winger,

connect with the full-back,

play behind the defence,

or move into the penalty area.

The half-space gives a creative midfielder multiple directions of attack.

That makes it one of the most valuable areas for the modern No.8.

---

# 6. Ødegaard — The Half-Space Playmaker

Martin Ødegaard is a good example of the bridge between a No.8 and No.10.

He frequently operates in the right half-space.

```text
          ST

               RW
        ØDEGAARD

           CM
```

From this position, he can combine with the winger.

Find the striker.

Connect with an overlapping player.

Move into the box.

Or recycle possession and begin another attack.

The important point is that Ødegaard is not simply standing in a central No.10 position waiting for the ball.

He helps **shape the attack from a specific channel**.

His role shows how modern playmaking can become more spatial and less positionally fixed.

---

# 7. Bellingham — When the No.8 Becomes an Attacker

Jude Bellingham represents another evolution.

He is not simply a passer.

He can begin deeper and carry the attack forward himself.

```text
START
  ↓
No.8
  ↓
CARRY
  ↓
BOX
```

One of his most dangerous qualities is the **Late Arrival**.

Imagine the striker occupying the centre-backs.

```text
        ST
        ↓
     CB   CB

       SPACE
         ↑
    BELLINGHAM
```

The defenders are already focused on the forward line.

Then Bellingham arrives from midfield.

That movement can be difficult to track because he was not standing in the attacking line from the beginning.

The classic No.10 often **started** behind the striker.

A player like Bellingham can **arrive** behind the striker.

That difference matters.

---

# 8. What Changed From No.10 to No.8?

The biggest change is the range of responsibility.

### Classic No.10

```text
Receive High
     ↓
Turn
     ↓
Create Final Action
```

### Modern Creative No.8

```text
Receive Deeper
     ↓
Progress the Ball
     ↓
Connect Midfield
     ↓
Enter Half-Space
     ↓
Create / Finish
```

The No.8 can participate in more phases of the attack.

Build-up.

Progression.

Connection.

Chance creation.

Box arrival.

Playmaking has therefore expanded from **one dangerous zone into several different zones**.

---

# 9. Stop Asking: "Who Is the No.10?"

This leads to a better way of watching modern football.

Instead of asking:

**"Who is playing No.10?"**

Ask:

**"Who is carrying the team's creativity?"**

Sometimes it is a No.8.

Sometimes a winger.

Sometimes a full-back.

Sometimes several players share the responsibility.

The shirt number and starting position matter less than the function.

And central midfield remains one of the best places to control that function.

Why?

Because from there, the player can see and influence more of the pitch.

---

# Conclusion — Playmaking Moved Back to See More

The playmaker did not move from No.10 to No.8 because creativity became less important.

In many ways, the opposite happened.

The playmaker moved deeper because he could influence **more of the game**.

Higher up, you are closer to goal.

Deeper, you can see the whole attack developing.

Modrić used scanning, receiving and turning to connect phases.

Kroos controlled direction and tempo from deeper areas.

Ødegaard uses the half-space to shape attacks.

Bellingham can begin as a midfielder and finish the move inside the box.

The modern playmaker is therefore not simply the player who makes the final pass.

He can:

**Build. Connect. Progress. Create. Arrive.**

The classic No.10 often controlled the final chapter of the attack.

The creative No.8 can help write the story from the beginning.

The playmaker did not disappear.

**He moved a few steps deeper so he could see more.**
