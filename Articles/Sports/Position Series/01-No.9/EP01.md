# EP01 — Why Are Elite No.9s So Rare Today?

## How the Traditional Centre-Forward Became Football’s Rarest Elite Position

### The Evolution of Football Positions — No.9

There was a time when almost every major European club seemed to have a world-class centre-forward.

In the early 2000s, football had an extraordinary collection of strikers:

**Ronaldo Nazário. Thierry Henry. Christian Vieri. Andriy Shevchenko. Hernán Crespo. Ruud van Nistelrooy. Filippo Inzaghi. David Trezeguet. Raúl. Fernando Morientes.**

And that is only part of the list.

The remarkable thing was not simply that these players existed at the same time.

It was that some clubs could have **two or three elite forwards in the same squad**.

AC Milan could field Shevchenko and Inzaghi.

Inter had Vieri and Crespo.

Real Madrid had Ronaldo, Raúl and Morientes across the period.

Juventus could rely on Trezeguet alongside Alessandro Del Piero.

The problem for elite clubs was often not:

**“Where can we find a striker?”**

It was:

**“Which of our strikers should start?”**

Twenty years later, the situation feels very different.

Many of Europe's richest and most sophisticated clubs have spent years searching for a reliable elite centre-forward.

Barcelona invested in Robert Lewandowski even when he was already in his thirties.

Arsenal spent several seasons debating whether they needed a true goalscoring No.9 before eventually moving for Viktor Gyökeres.

Real Madrid have assembled extraordinary attacking talent, but much of their modern attack has been built around mobile forwards rather than the classic penalty-box No.9 who permanently occupies the centre-backs.

So what happened?

Did football simply stop producing great strikers?

Did youth academies forget how to develop them?

Or did modern tactics make the traditional No.9 obsolete?

The answer is more interesting.

**The No.9 did not disappear.**

**The job became much harder.**

---

# 1. The Golden Age of the Specialist

To understand today's shortage, we first need to understand what a No.9 used to be.

The traditional centre-forward was a specialist.

His main responsibility was brutally simple:

## Score goals.

A classic attacking structure might look like this:

```text
LW  ──────────── CF ──────────── RW
                 ●
            CB       CB
```

The No.9 operated around the centre-backs.

He attacked crosses.

He made runs behind the defensive line.

He attacked rebounds.

He challenged for headers.

He protected the ball with his back to goal.

He attacked the near post.

He attacked the far post.

And most importantly:

**he finished chances.**

A striker could have relatively little involvement in possession and still produce a decisive performance.

For the traditional No.9, the defining question was:

> **Can you score?**

Everything else was secondary.

---

# 2. Football Used to Need More Strikers

There is also a simple mathematical explanation for why the early 2000s appeared to have so many centre-forwards.

Football used more of them.

Two-striker formations were common.

Consider the traditional 4-4-2:

```text
             ST1        ST2

LM        CM                CM        RM

LB        CB                CB        RB
```

Or the 4-3-1-2:

```text
             ST        ST

                  AM

          CM      CM      CM
```

Both systems require **two starting forwards**.

Imagine 20 major clubs using two-striker systems.

That creates approximately:

**20 clubs × 2 strikers = 40 starting striker positions.**

Now move those same clubs into a 4-3-3:

```text
LW              CF              RW
```

Suddenly:

**20 clubs × 1 CF = 20 starting centre-forward positions.**

This matters.

When football provides fewer specialist striker positions at the highest level, fewer young players are developed exclusively for those positions.

The tactical ecosystem changed.

---

# 3. Two Strikers Could Divide the Work

There was another advantage to the old system.

A striker did not need to do everything.

In a two-forward system, responsibilities could be divided.

```text
TARGET MAN             MOBILE STRIKER
Strength               Movement
Hold-up                Channels
Headers                Creativity
Duels                  Runs
```

Or:

```text
FINISHER               CREATOR
```

One player could stay high.

The other could drop deeper.

One could attack the box.

The other could connect midfield and attack.

This created an environment where specialists could flourish.

Filippo Inzaghi did not need to become an elite playmaker.

David Trezeguet did not need to operate like a midfielder.

Ruud van Nistelrooy did not need to spend the entire match rotating into wide areas.

Their specialization was not considered a weakness.

It was their value.

---

# 4. Then Football Moved Toward 4-3-3

The tactical landscape gradually changed.

The dominant structure moved from systems such as:

**4-4-2**

toward:

**4-3-3**

and

**4-2-3-1.**

This produced a fundamental change.

Instead of two strikers sharing responsibilities, there was often only one.

```text
LW              CF              RW

                AM

          CM          CM
```

That single centre-forward now had to satisfy the needs of the entire attacking system.

And coaches began asking him to do more.

Much more.

---

# 5. The Modern No.9 Has an Enormous Job Description

The modern elite centre-forward is expected to:

score,

press,

link play,

hold the ball,

run behind,

attack crosses,

combine with midfielders,

create space for wingers,

occupy centre-backs,

counter-press,

defend passing lanes,

make channel runs,

and sometimes drop into midfield.

So the ideal modern striker almost needs:

**Vieri's strength**

*

**Inzaghi's movement**

*

**Henry's speed**

*

**Bergkamp's link play**

*

**Tevez's pressing**

*

**Ronaldo's finishing**

in one player.

That is an absurdly demanding profile.

And naturally, very few players can satisfy it.

---

# 6. The False 9 Revolution Changed Everything

Then came one of the most important tactical developments of modern football:

## The False 9.

The traditional No.9 behaved roughly like this:

```text
        CB       CF       CB
```

He occupied the centre-backs.

The False 9 did something different.

```text
        CB                CB

                 ↓
                CF

            CM       CM
```

He moved away from them.

This created a defensive dilemma.

If a centre-back followed:

```text
CB
 ↓
False 9

        SPACE
          ↑
       Winger
```

space appeared behind him.

If the defender stayed:

```text
        CB             CB

             False 9
                ●

              FREE
```

the False 9 could receive between the lines.

Lionel Messi under Pep Guardiola demonstrated how devastating this concept could become.

Suddenly, one of football's biggest tactical questions became:

> **Do we actually need a traditional striker?**

---

# 7. The Goals Moved to the Wings

Another revolution was happening simultaneously.

Traditionally, the winger created goals.

The striker scored them.

```text
WINGER
   ↓
CROSS
   ↓
NO.9
   ↓
GOAL
```

Then players such as Cristiano Ronaldo and Lionel Messi changed expectations.

Wide attackers became primary goalscorers.

Later generations continued the trend.

Mohamed Salah.

Sadio Mané.

Son Heung-min.

Vinícius Júnior.

The attacking structure increasingly became:

```text
        LW        CF        RW
         ↓                   ↓

       GOALS               GOALS
```

The centre-forward was no longer automatically the team's dominant scorer.

This created the **Inside Forward era**.

And once wide players started scoring 20 or 30 goals, the CF could perform other tactical functions.

---

# 8. The Death of the Pure Poacher

This development created problems for another famous football archetype:

## The Poacher.

Imagine a striker who touches the ball only 15 times.

He contributes little to build-up.

He does not dribble.

He does not create chances.

He barely appears in midfield.

But in the 82nd minute:

```text
Cross
  ↓
Deflection
  ↓
STRIKER
  ↓
GOAL
```

For a classic poacher, that could be a successful performance.

Today, a coach may ask:

What did he do during the press?

Did he block the opposition No.6?

Did he provide an outlet?

Did he counter-press?

Did he create space for the winger?

Did he connect midfield and attack?

Did he help us escape pressure?

Modern football increasingly judges strikers across the entire game rather than only inside the penalty area.

That makes the pure poacher much harder to accommodate.

---

# 9. Pressing Turned the Striker Into a Defender

Perhaps no tactical development changed the position more than pressing.

The modern striker is the team's **first defender**.

Suppose the opposition begins building:

```text
          CB1          CB2

                DM
```

The CF cannot simply wait.

His pressing angle matters.

```text
              CF
             ↙

          CB1          CB2

                DM
```

He can pressure CB1 while using his body to block the passing lane toward the defensive midfielder.

The rest of the team responds to his movement.

```text
             CF  ↘

        CB1          CB2
                       ↓
                    PRESS TRAP
```

The No.9 is therefore not merely chasing defenders.

He is helping **design the team's defensive structure**.

A striker now requires defensive intelligence.

---

# 10. The Modern No.9 Must Also Think Like a Midfielder

When his team has possession, the requirements change again.

Against a compact defence:

```text
       ● ● ● ●
       ● ● ● ●

          CF
```

there may be no useful space between the centre-backs.

So the striker drops.

```text
       ● ● ● ●

           CF
           ↓

          CM
```

Now he receives between the lines.

He must scan.

Control under pressure.

Protect the ball.

Recognize the third-man run.

Play one-touch combinations.

Then immediately attack the box again.

This creates one of the defining contradictions of the modern position:

> **The modern No.9 must think like a midfielder and finish like a striker.**

---

# 11. Youth Development Changed Too

The tactical revolution eventually reached football academies.

Traditional striker development placed enormous emphasis on:

**Box Movement**

**One-Touch Finishing**

**Heading**

**Rebounds**

**Near-Post Runs**

**Far-Post Runs**

**Blind-Side Movement**

**Centre-Back Manipulation**

**Offside-Line Timing**

Modern academies increasingly value:

**Technical Ability**

**Possession**

**Pressing**

**Position Rotation**

**Versatility**

**Combination Play**

**Tactical Intelligence**

These are positive developments.

They produce more complete footballers.

But they may also produce fewer extreme specialists.

A technically gifted young attacker may become:

a winger,

an attacking midfielder,

a False 9,

or a hybrid forward

rather than spending ten years learning the tiny details of penalty-box movement.

---

# 12. Elite Finishing Is More Than Shooting Technique

This is important because elite No.9 play is often misunderstood.

The difference between an average striker and a world-class striker is not simply shooting accuracy.

Consider this situation:

```text
       CB             CB

              CF
```

An average striker watches the ball.

An elite striker watches:

**the ball + defender + space.**

Then he moves toward the defender's blind side.

```text
       CB             CB
                          CF
```

The cross begins.

The striker moves first.

```text
       CB          CB
                      ↘
                       CF → Near Post
```

The difference may be half a second.

But half a second is enormous inside the penalty area.

World-class No.9s master:

**Timing**

**Anticipation**

**Blind-Side Movement**

**Defender Manipulation**

**Body Orientation**

**Rebound Prediction**

**Composure**

**Spatial Awareness**

The final shot may be easy.

Creating the opportunity to take that easy shot is the difficult part.

---

# 13. Why Haaland-Type Players Are So Valuable

This brings us to modern elite strikers.

A player such as Erling Haaland possesses a rare combination:

**Physical Power**

*

**Explosive Depth Running**

*

**Elite Box Movement**

*

**High-Level Finishing**

*

**Constant Goal Threat**

The important word is **combination**.

Many forwards are fast.

Many are strong.

Many finish well.

Many press intelligently.

Many link play effectively.

Very few combine enough elite qualities to lead the attack of a Champions League-level club.

Economically, this creates a simple problem:

```text
Supply of Elite No.9s
          ↓↓↓

Demand from Elite Clubs
          ↑↑↑

Market Value
          ↑↑↑
```

The scarcity is structural.

---

# 14. Barcelona and Lewandowski Show the Value of a Finished No.9

Barcelona's move for Robert Lewandowski was a perfect illustration.

They were signing an older player.

But they were also purchasing something extremely difficult to develop:

## A finished elite striker.

Lewandowski already understood:

when to attack the near post,

when to delay his run,

when to drop,

when to pin a centre-back,

how to receive with his back to goal,

how to finish with either foot,

how to attack aerial balls,

how to combine,

and how to manipulate defenders.

That is decades of striker knowledge contained in one player.

The value was not simply his finishing.

It was his complete understanding of the position.

---

# 15. Arsenal's Striker Search Reveals the Same Problem

Arsenal offer another useful example.

A team can possess:

excellent build-up,

excellent midfield control,

excellent pressing,

dangerous wingers,

strong defensive organization,

and sophisticated positional play.

But eventually every attack reaches one final question:

```text
Build-Up
   ↓
Progression
   ↓
Chance Creation
   ↓
   ?
   ↓
GOAL
```

**Who finishes the move?**

That final step can determine the difference between domination and victory.

The search for players such as Viktor Gyökeres reflects the continuing value of a forward whose greatest instinct remains:

**attack the goal.**

---

# 16. Real Madrid Demonstrate Another Solution

Modern teams do not always solve the No.9 shortage by finding a traditional No.9.

Sometimes they redesign the attack.

Real Madrid can use highly mobile forwards who exchange positions and attack space.

A player such as Kylian Mbappé is devastating when space appears:

```text
        SPACE
          ↓

Mbappé → → → → GOAL
```

Compare that with the traditional reference striker:

```text
        CB     No.9     CB
```

The classic No.9 permanently occupies central defenders.

The mobile forward may start centrally but move left, run channels, exchange positions, or attack from deeper areas.

This represents another important modern development:

**Instead of finding one player who performs every No.9 function, teams can distribute those functions across several attackers.**

---

# 17. Compare That With the Early 2000s

Now return to the old environment.

AC Milan could have:

**Shevchenko + Inzaghi**

Inter could have:

**Vieri + Crespo**

Real Madrid could call upon:

**Ronaldo + Raúl + Morientes**

The tactical challenge could actually be:

> **How do we fit all these great forwards into the team?**

Today, the challenge for many clubs is almost the opposite:

> **Where can we find one complete elite centre-forward?**

That contrast tells us something important.

This is not simply about individual generations.

The football ecosystem itself changed.

---

Look at the broader transformation:

---

# 19. The No.10 Suffered the Same Fate

There is an interesting parallel.

The classic No.10 once had a specialized job.

Create.

Players such as:

Zidane,

Riquelme,

Aimar,

Rui Costa,

Totti

could be given significant attacking freedom.

Modern football increasingly asks attacking midfielders to:

press,

cover,

rotate,

track runners,

defend space,

and contribute physically.

The specialist No.10 became harder to accommodate.

The same thing happened to the specialist No.9.

Modern football increasingly prefers:

```text
SPECIALIST
     ↓
MULTI-FUNCTIONAL PLAYER
```

And this is one of the central themes of football's tactical evolution.

---

# 20. The Modern No.9 Is Actually Several Positions in One

Consider the striker's responsibilities across different phases.

### During Build-Up

```text
CB → CM → CF ↓
             ↓
          LINK PLAY
```

He becomes a connector.

### During High Press

```text
CF → CB
```

He becomes the first defender.

### During Transition

```text
CF → → → → SPACE
```

He becomes a runner.

### Against a Deep Defence

```text
          CF
       CB    CB
```

He becomes a target man.

### Inside the Penalty Area

```text
Cross → CF → GOAL
```

He becomes a poacher.

One player may therefore need to function as:

**Poacher**

*

**Target Man**

*

**Pressing Forward**

*

**False 9**

*

**Channel Runner**

*

**Link Player**

depending on the situation.

That is the modern No.9.

---

# 21. Why the Standard Became So Difficult

The evolution becomes clearer when we compare expectations.

| Skill                 | Traditional No.9 | Modern Elite No.9 |
| --------------------- | ---------------: | ----------------: |
| Finishing             |            ★★★★★ |             ★★★★★ |
| Box Movement          |            ★★★★★ |             ★★★★★ |
| Heading               |            ★★★★★ |             ★★★★☆ |
| Hold-Up Play          |            ★★★★☆ |             ★★★★☆ |
| Link Play             |            ★★★☆☆ |             ★★★★★ |
| Pressing              |            ★★☆☆☆ |             ★★★★★ |
| Mobility              |            ★★★☆☆ |             ★★★★★ |
| Tactical Intelligence |            ★★★★☆ |             ★★★★★ |
| Defensive Work        |            ★★☆☆☆ |             ★★★★☆ |
| Position Rotation     |            ★★☆☆☆ |             ★★★★★ |

This is the key.

**The examination became harder.**

A player who would once have been considered an excellent specialist striker may now be considered tactically incomplete.

---

# 22. But Were the Old Strikers Simply Better?

Not necessarily.

This is where nostalgia can become misleading.

The early 2000s unquestionably produced an extraordinary generation of centre-forwards.

But the tactical environment also gave those players more opportunities to specialize.

Modern football still produces extraordinary goalscorers.

They simply appear in different positions.

A player who might once have been classified as a second striker can now become an inside forward.

The traditional distribution was:

```text
          ★ No.9 ★
```

The modern distribution can be:

```text
★ LW       CF       RW ★
```

The goalscorer did not disappear.

## The goals moved.

---

# 23. And Now the No.9 Is Coming Back

Football evolves through action and reaction.

First came the traditional striker.

Then teams discovered False 9s.

Then fluid front threes became common.

Then defences adapted.

Central blocks became more compact.

Spaces between midfield and defence became smaller.

Pressing systems became more organized.

And suddenly, having a physically dominant striker capable of occupying two centre-backs became extremely useful again.

The cycle looks like this:

```text
Traditional No.9
       ↓
False 9 Revolution
       ↓
Fluid Front Three
       ↓
Defensive Adaptation
       ↓
Need for Penalty-Box Presence
       ↓
RETURN OF THE No.9
```

The difference is that the returning No.9 cannot simply be an old-fashioned poacher.

He must bring old-school goalscoring qualities into the modern tactical environment.

Put them together:

```text
OLD-SCHOOL KILLER
        +
```

That player is extraordinarily difficult to produce.

Which is precisely why every elite club wants one.

---

# 25. The No.9 Paradox

This brings us to the central paradox of the modern centre-forward.

In the early 2000s, the question was:

## “Can you score?”

During the False 9 revolution, the question briefly became:

## “Do we even need a striker?”

Today the question is:

## “Can we find a striker who does everything?”

The modern No.9 must answer:

**Can you score?**

**Can you press?**

**Can you link play?**

**Can you run behind?**

**Can you hold the ball?**

**Can you create space?**

**Can you attack crosses?**

**Can you play against a low block?**

**Can you lead the press?**

**Can you participate in build-up?**

**Can you still be inside the box when the final pass arrives?**

Very few players can answer **yes** to all of them.

---

# Conclusion — The No.9 Did Not Die

The disappearance of elite No.9s is not really a story about declining talent.

It is a story about **rising expectations**.

The traditional striker was a specialist.

The modern striker is a multi-functional system player who must still possess the ruthless instincts of the old specialist.

That is why the position feels so scarce.

Twenty years ago, many clubs could accommodate a striker who was exceptional at three or four things.

Today, an elite club may demand excellence across eight or ten different areas.

So perhaps we have been asking the wrong question.

Instead of:

> **“Why doesn't modern football produce as many great No.9s?”**

we should ask:

> **“How many players in football history could actually satisfy everything we now demand from a modern No.9?”**

That changes the perspective completely.

The No.9 is not dead.

The position has become harder to master.

And because genuine elite No.9s are becoming harder to find, their tactical and economic value may be rising again.
