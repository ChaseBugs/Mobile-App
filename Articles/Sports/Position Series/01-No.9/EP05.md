# EP05 — The Return of the No.9

## Why Modern Football Needed the Striker Again

### The Evolution of Football Positions — No.9

For a period, modern football seemed to be moving in one clear direction.

Strikers dropped deeper.

Wingers became primary goalscorers.

Midfielders attacked the box.

Forwards created space instead of simply occupying it.

**False 9.**

**Inside Forward.**

**Fluid Front Three.**

**Position Rotation.**

From that perspective, the traditional No.9 appeared to be losing his place.

Then something changed.

Elite teams began searching again for a very specific kind of player.

A striker who could:

**Pin centre-backs.**

**Attack crosses.**

**Threaten the space behind.**

**Occupy the penalty area.**

**Win direct duels.**

**Finish low-quality chances.**

**Give the attack a permanent central reference.**

In other words:

# FOOTBALL NEEDED THE No.9 AGAIN.

Haaland.

Lewandowski.

Kane.

Isak.

Gyökeres.

Osimhen.

The striker had not disappeared.

But this was not simply a return to the centre-forward of the 1990s or 2000s.

Modern football demanded more.

```text
OLD-SCHOOL KILLER
        +
MODERN TEAM PLAYER
        =
COMPLETE MODERN No.9
```

The No.9 returned.

But he returned in a new form.

---

# 1. Why Did Football Need the No.9 Again?

Football tactics evolve through a constant cycle.

```text
NEW IDEA
   ↓
SUCCESS
   ↓
DEFENSIVE ADAPTATION
   ↓
NEW PROBLEM
   ↓
NEW SOLUTION
```

The False 9 followed exactly this pattern.

Originally, withdrawing the striker away from the centre-backs created enormous problems.

A defender had to decide:

Follow him?

Or stay?

If the centre-back followed:

```text
CB ↑ follows False 9

      SPACE
        ↓
   WINGER RUNS
```

space appeared behind.

If he stayed:

```text
CB stays

False 9 receives
      ↓
MIDFIELD OVERLOAD
```

the attacking team gained an extra midfielder.

For a time, this dilemma was devastating.

But defences adapted.

Centre-backs became more disciplined.

Holding midfielders protected the space between the lines.

Back fours became better at maintaining their shape.

Compact blocks became increasingly sophisticated.

```text
        DM

     CM    CM

LB    CB  CB    RB
```

The centre-back no longer had to follow every dropping striker.

The midfield could absorb him.

And once that happened, another question became important:

# WHO OCCUPIES THE BOX?

---

# 2. Creating Space Is Not the Same as Scoring

The False 9 revolution taught football how to create space.

But the objective of football never changed.

# SCORE.

Space is useful only when somebody attacks it.

```text
CREATE SPACE
     ↓
ATTACK SPACE
     ↓
CREATE CHANCE
     ↓
FINISH
```

If the centre-forward drops deep,

the wingers remain wide,

and the midfielders stay outside the penalty area,

the attack can produce something strange.

Beautiful possession.

Excellent spacing.

Good circulation.

But:

```text
          GOAL

     CB          CB

          EMPTY
```

no central presence.

That became one of the contradictions of highly fluid attacking football.

Teams became excellent at moving defenders.

But sometimes nobody was positioned to punish the movement.

Modern football eventually rediscovered a simple truth:

# THE BOX STILL NEEDS AN OCCUPANT.

---

# 3. Low Blocks Brought the No.9 Back Into Focus

Against elite possession teams, many opponents defend with a deep and compact block.

```text
████████████████

      LOW BLOCK

████████████████
```

There may be almost no space behind.

```text
LB   CB   CB   RB
   DM   DM   DM
```

Against this structure, a False 9 dropping deeper may not create much disruption.

The centre-backs can stay.

The defensive midfielders can step toward the ball.

The team remains compact.

So the attacking team must solve a different problem.

Not:

# HOW DO WE ATTACK SPACE BEHIND?

but:

# HOW DO WE SCORE WHEN THERE IS ALMOST NO SPACE?

That increases the importance of:

**Crosses**

**Cutbacks**

**Near-post runs**

**Far-post runs**

**Second balls**

**Rebounds**

**Aerial duels**

**One-touch finishes**

And all of these actions reward one thing:

# PENALTY-AREA PRESENCE.

---

# 4. Pinning Centre-Backs Became Valuable Again

One of the most underrated qualities of a No.9 is that he can change the opponent's structure without touching the ball.

Imagine one striker standing between two centre-backs.

```text
CB       ST       CB
```

The defenders cannot simply step forward whenever they want.

Why?

Because one bad step can expose the space behind.

```text
CB ↑ step?

        ST
         ↓
      DEPTH
```

The striker therefore **pins** them.

This matters because defensive lines and midfield lines are connected.

If the centre-backs stay deeper:

```text
MIDFIELD

     SPACE

CB    ST    CB
```

the gap between midfield and defence can increase.

That gives attacking midfielders more room.

So the striker can create space simply by remaining in position.

# A No.9 CAN CREATE SPACE BY REFUSING TO LEAVE IT.

---

# 5. Pinning — A Core Skill of the Modern No.9

Pinning means positioning yourself so that a defender cannot comfortably leave his zone.

```text
        ST
      ↙    ↘

    CB      CB
```

Both centre-backs must remain aware of the striker.

That can free another player.

```text
        ST

     CB    CB

       SPACE

        AM
```

The No.10 receives between the lines.

Or the winger can attack inside.

```text
         ST

CB               CB

LW → HALF-SPACE
```

The striker has created the advantage without receiving a pass.

This is fundamental.

A strong No.9 does not only create space through movement.

He can create space by:

# PREVENTING DEFENDERS FROM MOVING.

---

# 6. False 9 vs Traditional 9 — Opposite Methods, Same Objective

The False 9 and traditional No.9 appear to be opposites.

But tactically, they are trying to achieve the same thing.

Manipulate defenders.

### FALSE 9

```text
LEAVE SPACE
    ↓
DEFENDER FOLLOWS
    ↓
SPACE OPENS BEHIND
```

### TRADITIONAL No.9

```text
OCCUPY SPACE
     ↓
DEFENDER STAYS
     ↓
SPACE OPENS ELSEWHERE
```

One manipulates by leaving.

The other manipulates by staying.

# MOVEMENT CREATES SPACE.

But:

# OCCUPATION CAN ALSO CREATE SPACE.

This is one reason the No.9 became tactically relevant again.

---

# 7. The No.9 Gives the Attack a Reference Point

Fluidity is powerful.

But too much fluidity can create uncertainty.

If everyone moves:

Who stays?

If everyone comes toward the ball:

Who attacks behind?

If everyone creates:

Who finishes?

The No.9 can answer these questions.

```text
WINGER   AM   WINGER
    \     |     /
        No.9
         ↓
       GOAL
```

The striker becomes:

# THE REFERENCE POINT.

He gives teammates something around which they can organise their movements.

The winger knows where to cross.

The No.10 knows where the centre-backs are occupied.

The full-back knows where the far-post target may arrive.

The midfielder knows who can stretch the line.

The No.9 therefore provides not only goals.

He provides:

# ATTACKING CLARITY.

---

# 8. Depth Threat Changes the Defensive Line

Another major weapon is **Depth Threat**.

The striker threatens to run behind the defensive line.

```text
DEFENSIVE LINE
──────────────

        ST
        ↓↓↓

      SPACE
```

A fast striker changes the defender's decision.

The centre-back thinks:

Can I step forward?

Can I squeeze the midfield?

Can I leave space behind me?

If the striker is dangerous enough:

```text
CB DROPS
   ↓
MIDFIELD SPACE INCREASES
```

That means pace affects more than the individual duel.

# SPEED CHANGES THE GEOMETRY OF THE PITCH.

A striker may never receive the through ball.

The possibility of the run can already force the defence deeper.

---

# 9. The High-Line Era Created the Perfect Environment for Depth Runners

Elite teams increasingly defend high because a high defensive line supports pressing.

```text
PRESS

MIDFIELD

HIGH LINE
────────────

     SPACE
```

The team becomes compact.

The distances between forwards, midfielders and defenders become shorter.

This makes counterpressing easier.

But it creates one major risk.

# SPACE BEHIND.

If the first press is broken:

```text
PRESS BEATEN
     ↓
THROUGH BALL
     ↓
FAST No.9
     ↓
1v1
```

the striker suddenly has a huge attacking area.

Therefore, the modern No.9 is increasingly asked to combine two qualities that once belonged to different striker types.

# BOX FINISHER

*

# DEPTH RUNNER

He must be dangerous when the opponent sits deep.

And dangerous when the opponent defends high.

# 10. The Modern No.9 Is Not a Static Target Man

The return of the striker does not mean the return of a stationary big man waiting for long balls.

The old stereotype is:

```text
LONG BALL
    ↓
BIG STRIKER
    ↓
HEADER
```

The modern No.9 is far more mobile.

His movement may look like:

```text
PIN CB
   ↓
CHANNEL RUN
   ↓
DROP
   ↓
LINK PLAY
   ↓
RETURN TO BOX
```

The important skill is not simply movement.

It is:

# MOVEMENT TIMING.

When should he stay?

When should he leave?

When should he drop?

When should he attack the channel?

When should he arrive inside the box?

The modern No.9 does not move constantly.

He moves **purposefully**.

# 11. The Cutback Era Made Striker Movement Valuable Again

Modern attacking football relies heavily on reaching the byline and playing low balls backward.

```text
WINGER
   ↓
BYLINE
   ↓
CUTBACK
   ↓
FINISH
```

This creates several finishing zones.

```text
       GOAL

NEAR POST

SIX-YARD AREA

PENALTY SPOT

CUTBACK ZONE
```

An elite striker reads which space is opening.

He does not simply sprint toward goal.

Sometimes he attacks the near post.

Sometimes he delays.

Sometimes he pulls away toward the far post.

Sometimes the best movement is actually backward.

```text
WINGER → BYLINE

          ST
          ↓
    PENALTY SPOT
```

This looks modern.

But the skill itself is ancient.

# POACHING.

The irony is clear:

The more sophisticated attacking systems became,

the more valuable elite penalty-area instincts became.

# 12. The Modern No.9 Must Understand the Box

The penalty area is not one space.

It contains multiple zones.

A striker must constantly decide:

Where will the next ball arrive?

Where is the defender looking?

Which run creates separation?

Which zone is already occupied?

```text
            GOAL

     NP             FP

          6-YARD

        PENALTY SPOT

        CUTBACK ZONE
```

The best strikers do not react only after the cross is played.

They read the situation beforehand.

# SCAN

→

# ANTICIPATE

→

# SEPARATE

→

# ARRIVE

→

# FINISH

This is why finishing is not simply shooting technique.

Much of elite finishing happens:

# BEFORE THE BALL ARRIVES.

# 13. Erling Haaland — Manchester City

## From Positional Control to Ruthless Occupation

Haaland is one of the clearest symbols of the return of the No.9.

Manchester City were already one of Europe's most sophisticated possession teams before his arrival.

They could:

control possession,

create overloads,

move opponents,

rotate positions,

use false forwards,

and generate cutbacks.

What Haaland added was different.

# PERMANENT CENTRAL THREAT.

```text
CB       HAALAND       CB
```

The centre-backs always have a reference behind them.

If they step:

```text
CB ↑

      HAALAND
          ↓
        RUN
```

he attacks the depth.

If they stay:

```text
CB   HAALAND   CB

      MIDFIELD
        SPACE
```

City's creators can operate in front of them.

Haaland therefore gives City two simultaneous threats:

# DEPTH + BOX OCCUPATION.

In his first Manchester City season, 2022/23, he scored **52 goals in 53 matches in all competitions**, including a then-record **36 Premier League goals**, as City won the Premier League, FA Cup and their first Champions League to complete the Treble.

The important point is not that Haaland alone created that success.

City were already elite.

The tactical change was that their possession now had a permanent finishing reference.

# CITY BEFORE:

CONTROL → CREATE

# CITY WITH HAALAND:

CONTROL → CREATE → OCCUPY → FINISH

Haaland did not replace positional football.

He gave positional football a target.

# 16. Alexander Isak — Mobility Without Losing No.9 Presence

## Newcastle United

Alexander Isak represents one of the most technically complete modern No.9 profiles.

He is not primarily a static target man.

He is not purely a penalty-box poacher.

And he is not a False 9.

He combines:

# MOBILITY

*

# TECHNIQUE

*

# CHANNEL RUNNING

*

# DRIBBLING

*

# FINISHING

His movement is difficult for centre-backs because he does not remain on one reference point.

```text
           ISAK
          ↙    ↘

   LEFT CHANNEL   RIGHT CHANNEL
          ↓
       BOX RETURN
```

He may initially occupy the centre.

Then drift into the left channel.

Receive to feet.

Turn.

Combine.

Then arrive again inside the box.

That gives defenders a difficult question.

# FOLLOW HIM?

If they follow:

space may open centrally.

# LEAVE HIM?

He can receive and turn.

Unlike some pure runners, Isak is comfortable creating his own shooting situation.

```text
RECEIVE
   ↓
TURN
   ↓
CARRY
   ↓
SHIFT DEFENDER
   ↓
FINISH
```

That makes him especially valuable against organised blocks.

He does not always need the final pass to create the shot.

### Isak's Newcastle Impact

Isak joined Newcastle in 2022.

His first Premier League season was interrupted by injury, but he still scored **10 league goals in 22 appearances**. He then produced **21 league goals in 2023/24** and **23 in 2024/25**, finishing second in the 2024/25 Golden Boot race behind Mohamed Salah.

Across three Newcastle seasons he scored **62 goals in 109 appearances**, including **54 in 86 Premier League matches**.

His final Newcastle campaign showed the development clearly.

Newcastle finished **fifth in the Premier League in 2024/25**, with Isak their leading league scorer on 23.

The tactical significance of Isak was that Newcastle did not need to use him only as a stationary box reference.

He could become:

# No.9 → CHANNEL FORWARD → BALL CARRIER → No.9

within the same attack.

That mobility creates a different type of striker threat.

Haaland often forces the defensive line backward.

Isak can also pull individual defenders sideways.

```text
CB → FOLLOW ISAK WIDE

        CENTRAL SPACE
              ↓
      MIDFIELDER / WINGER
```

He manipulates the defensive line horizontally as well as vertically.

That is why his profile became so valuable.

In September 2025, Isak left Newcastle for Liverpool after establishing himself as one of the Premier League's leading forwards. Liverpool's official announcement recorded 62 goals in 109 Newcastle appearances and described him as adding elite forward quality to the reigning Premier League champions.

Isak therefore represents:

# THE MOBILE COMPLETE No.9.

Not only:

**Where can I finish?**

But:

**Where can I move to destabilise the centre-back before I finish?**

# 23. The Best No.9 Does Not Need Many Touches

Modern possession analysis often focuses on players who touch the ball frequently.

But a striker can dominate a match with relatively little possession.

Why?

Because his movements influence decisions.

```text

  ↓
CB DROPS
  ↓
AM RECEIVES

ST PINS
  ↓
CB STAYS
  ↓
WINGER ENTERS HALF-SPACE
```

The striker may not receive either pass.

But he helped create both situations.

This is why analysing a No.9 only through:

**Touches**

**Passes**

**Dribbles**

can be misleading.

You also need to observe:

# DEFENDER REACTION.

# 28. Why the No.9 Returned

The No.9 returned because several modern tactical developments accidentally recreated the conditions in which a striker becomes extremely valuable.

### LOW BLOCKS

created the need for box presence.

### HIGH LINES

created space for depth runners.

### INVERTED WINGERS

created greater central combinations but often reduced natural box occupation.

### ATTACKING FULL-BACKS

increased crossing and cutback opportunities.

### POSITIONAL PLAY

created high-quality spaces that needed a final occupant.

### HIGH PRESSING

increased the value of a striker who can provide a direct escape route.

So modern football did not move backward.

It moved forward until it discovered a new reason to use an old position.

# 29. The No.9 Did Not Defeat the False 9

This is perhaps the most important conclusion.

The return of the No.9 does not mean:

# FALSE 9 FAILED.

The False 9 permanently changed football.

It taught teams that the striker could manipulate space by leaving the centre.

Modern No.9s simply added another lesson:

# THE STRIKER CAN ALSO MANIPULATE SPACE BY OCCUPYING THE CENTRE.

The two ideas coexist.

Kane can drop like a False 9.

Then attack the box like a classic striker.

Isak can move into a channel.

Then return to become the central finisher.

Haaland can remain high and create midfield space without touching the ball.

The role is no longer defined by one starting position.

It is defined by:

# THE PROBLEM THE STRIKER SOLVES.

# Conclusion — The Striker Never Really Died

Football never stopped needing goals.

What changed was the preferred method of producing them.

For a period, teams tried to create goals by removing the striker from traditional spaces.

The False 9 dropped.

The winger entered.

The midfielder arrived.

The full-back supplied width.

The box became fluid.

Then defences adapted.

Low blocks became compact.

High lines became aggressive.

Midfield zones became crowded.

And football rediscovered the value of a player who could stand in the centre of all that complexity and create certainty.

Haaland provides permanent depth and box occupation.

Lewandowski gives possession a finishing reference.

Kane combines striker and playmaker.

Isak combines mobility, technique and finishing.

Gyökeres combines progression, power and goals.

Osimhen combines explosive depth with penalty-area dominance.

They are very different players.

But they share one fundamental function.

# THEY GIVE THE ATTACK A CENTRE.

The No.9 did not return because football became less sophisticated.

He returned because football became sophisticated enough to understand that occupying space can be just as important as creating it.

The False 9 taught football:

# LEAVE THE SPACE TO CREATE SPACE.

The modern No.9 answered:

# OCCUPY THE SPACE TO CREATE SPACE ELSEWHERE.

And the best modern strikers can do both.
