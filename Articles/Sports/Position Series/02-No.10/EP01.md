# EP01 — What Happened to Football’s No.10?

## From Zidane, Totti and Kaká to the Death and Evolution of the Classic Playmaker

In football, the **No.10** has always been special.

No.1 usually makes us think of the goalkeeper.

No.9 represents the goalscorer — the striker.

But what about No.10?

For decades, No.10 was more than a shirt number.

It represented the most creative player on the team.

The player who operated behind the strikers.

The player who found small spaces between defenders.

The player who saw passes others could not see.

The player who could change the entire direction of a match with one touch.

The **playmaker**.

Zinedine Zidane.

Francesco Totti.

Kaká.

And before them, legends such as Roberto Baggio and Diego Maradona.

Their styles were very different, but they shared one important characteristic:

**The attack revolved around the No.10.**

Now look at a typical modern 4-3-3.

```text
          ST

LW                  RW

      CM        CM

           DM
```

Something is missing.

**There is no traditional No.10 position.**

So what happened?

Where did football's No.10 go?

---

# 1. First: No.10 Is Not Just a Shirt Number

When we talk about the No.10, we are not necessarily talking about the player wearing the number 10 shirt.

We are talking about a **role**.

The traditional No.10 usually operated between the midfield and the forwards.

```text
           ST

        ★ No.10

      CM       CM

           DM
```

Now look at the same position from the opponent's perspective.

```text
CB                 CB
---------------------
        ★ No.10
---------------------
CM                 CM
```

The No.10 is standing between the opponent's **midfield line and defensive line**.

This is called playing:

**Between the Lines.**

Why is this space so dangerous?

Because if the No.10 receives the ball here and turns toward goal, several attacking options immediately become available.

```text
          ST
          ↑
          │
LW ←─── No.10 ───→ RW
          │
          ↓
        SHOT
```

Pass left.

Pass right.

Play the striker through.

Dribble forward.

Shoot.

One good reception can create several attacking possibilities.

This is why the classic No.10 did not necessarily need extraordinary speed or physical strength.

The essential qualities were different:

**Vision**

**First Touch**

**Ball Control**

**Passing**

**Dribbling**

**Decision-Making**

And perhaps most importantly:

**Understanding Space.**

---

# 2. Zidane — Why the No.10 Needed Space

Zinedine Zidane is one of the easiest players to study if you want to understand the traditional playmaker.

Imagine Zidane receiving the ball.

An opposition midfielder approaches him.

Another player begins closing the space.

A normal player might simply pass backward.

Zidane could use his first touch, body positioning and technique to escape the pressure.

```text
          CB
           ↑

CM →    ZIDANE    ← CM

           ↓
       OPEN SPACE
```

But the important part is not simply that Zidane beat one opponent.

Something else happened.

**That opponent had to leave his original position to pressure Zidane.**

And when a player leaves his position, space appears somewhere else.

Imagine this sequence.

```text
1. Midfielder approaches Zidane

          CM
           ↓
        Zidane


2. Midfielder leaves his position

       [ SPACE ]


3. Another attacker enters it

          ST
          ↓
       [ SPACE ]
```

This is one of the secrets behind great playmakers.

Their dribbling is not only about beating opponents.

It is about **forcing the defensive structure to move**.

Zidane attracted opponents.

He escaped them.

Then he exploited the space their movement had created.

That is why giving a great No.10 time and space was so dangerous.

---

# 3. Totti — When the No.10 Became a Goalscorer

Then we come to Francesco Totti.

Totti showed that a No.10 did not have to be only a provider.

A more traditional playmaker might look something like this:

```text
          ST

          10
       ↙      ↘
    PASS      PASS
```

Totti could do much more.

```text
          ST
          ↕
        TOTTI
      ↙   ↓   ↘

   PASS  SHOT  RUN
```

He could create.

He could shoot.

He could enter the penalty area.

He could move closer to the striker.

And sometimes, he could become the striker himself.

This represented an important evolution.

**The line between the No.10 and the forward was beginning to disappear.**

The traditional playmaker often asked:

**“How can I create a goal for the striker?”**

A player such as Totti could ask:

**“Why can't I become the final attacker myself?”**

This idea would later become extremely important in roles such as the **False 9**.

---

# 4. Kaká — When Speed Was Added to the Playmaker

Then came another important player.

**Kaká.**

Kaká was not exactly the same type of classic No.10 as earlier playmakers.

And that is precisely why he is so important to this story.

He added another devastating weapon:

**Speed.**

A traditional playmaker's sequence could often look like this:

```text
RECEIVE
   ↓
TURN
   ↓
LOOK
   ↓
PASS
```

Kaká could do something different.

```text
RECEIVE
   ↓
TURN
   ↓
RUN
   ↓
RUN
   ↓
PASS / SHOOT
```

When he saw open space, he could attack it himself.

He could carry the ball through midfield.

He could run directly at the defensive line.

Then he could make the final pass or finish the attack himself.

This is known as:

**Ball Carrying.**

And it was becoming increasingly valuable.

---

# 5. Why Kaká Was Important to the Evolution of the No.10

Football was becoming faster.

Teams increasingly wanted to attack before the opposition could reorganize.

Imagine the following sequence:

```text
BALL WON
   ↓
PASS TO KAKÁ
   ↓
CARRY FORWARD
   ↓
DEFENCE NOT ORGANIZED
   ↓
PASS / SHOT
```

The playmaker was no longer only someone who **designed** an attack.

He could also be the player who **transported the attack forward**.

This is why Kaká is useful when studying the evolution of the No.10.

Zidane represented:

**Control + Technique + Vision**

Totti added:

**Goals + Forward Movement**

Kaká emphasized:

**Speed + Ball Carrying + Transition**

The No.10 was already changing.

---

# 6. Then Football Changed Around Them

The problem was not that talented playmakers suddenly disappeared.

The environment around them changed.

Remember what the classic No.10 wanted.

```text
DF                 DF


        No.10


CM                 CM
```

Receive the ball.

Turn.

Look forward.

Make a decision.

Simple.

But modern defensive systems increasingly looked more like this:

```text
DF      DF      DF
          ↓

       [No.10]

       ↑      ↑
CM         DM         CM
```

The moment the No.10 receives the ball, pressure arrives.

A defensive midfielder is in front.

A centre-back is behind.

Another midfielder closes from the side.

The two things the classic No.10 valued most begin to disappear:

**TIME + SPACE.**

---

# 7. The First Change — Compactness

One of the most important concepts in modern football is:

**Compactness.**

In simple terms, it means keeping the distances between players and defensive lines small.

Imagine the defensive structure is stretched.

```text
DF   DF   DF   DF


       ★ No.10


MF   MF   MF   MF
```

This is paradise for the No.10.

There is room to receive.

There is time to turn.

There is space to dribble.

Now compress the same structure.

```text
DF   DF   DF   DF
        ★10
MF   MF   MF   MF
```

Everything changes.

The No.10 receives the ball.

A midfielder can immediately press from behind.

A defender can step forward.

Another midfielder can close from the side.

The No.10 has nowhere to go.

The position where classic playmakers once lived became one of the most heavily protected areas of the pitch.

**Their natural habitat was disappearing.**

---

# 8. The Second Change — Pressing

Then came an even bigger problem.

**Pressing.**

Modern teams increasingly defend from the front.

That means attackers are also defenders.

Imagine this structure:

```text
            ST
             ↓

LW →       No.10       ← RW

         CM      CM
```

What happens if the No.10 does not press?

The opposition defensive midfielder may become free.

```text
CB ─────────→ DM
               ↓
               ↓
          FREE TO ADVANCE
```

Now the opponent has an easy escape route.

One player refusing to press can damage the entire pressing structure.

So coaches began demanding more from creative players.

It was no longer enough to say:

**“When we have the ball, create something.”**

Now the instruction became:

**“When we don't have the ball, you also have a job.”**

This was a major problem for the traditional **luxury playmaker**.

---

# 9. The Third Change — Transition

Another word beginners should learn is:

**Transition.**

Football is not simply divided into attack and defence.

There are crucial moments between them.

One of the most important is the moment immediately after losing possession.

Imagine your team attacking.

```text
          ST

LW       No.10       RW

      CM       CM
```

The No.10 attempts a pass.

It is intercepted.

Suddenly:

```text
        BALL
          ↓
          ↓
          ↓
   COUNTERATTACK
```

In older systems, the playmaker might remain forward and wait for the next attack.

Modern football often demands an immediate reaction.

**Lost the ball?**

Press.

**Opponent looking for a pass?**

Block the passing lane.

**Opponent escapes the press?**

Recover your defensive position.

Suddenly the requirements of a playmaker have changed.

The old checklist looked like this:

```text
CLASSIC No.10

Vision
Passing
Technique
Dribbling
Creativity
```

The modern checklist is much longer.

```text
MODERN PLAYMAKER

Vision
Passing
Technique
Dribbling
Creativity
+
Pressing
Running
Positioning
Transition
Defensive Work
```

The genius did not disappear.

**The genius was simply given more work to do.**

---

# 10. Then Look at the Rise of the 4-3-3

There is another obvious tactical change.

A 4-2-3-1 has a natural position for the No.10.

```text
           ST

LW        No.10        RW

       DM        DM
```

Now look at a 4-3-3.

```text
LW         ST         RW

      LCM      RCM

           DM
```

Where is the No.10?

**There isn't one.**

But does this mean creativity disappeared?

No.

Something much more interesting happened.

Creativity was redistributed.

The left central midfielder can create.

The right central midfielder can move between the lines.

A winger can move inside.

The striker can drop deeper.

A full-back can move into midfield.

The defensive midfielder can control the build-up.

In the past, we might have had:

```text
        ★ No.10
           ↓
      CREATIVITY
```

Now we can have:

```text
           ST
           ↕
LW ← CREATIVITY → RW
       ↕       ↕
      CM       CM
           ↕
           DM
```

**Creativity moved from one player to an entire system.**

---

# 11. But the 4-3-3 Did Not Kill the No.10

This is an important distinction.

It would be too simplistic to say:

**“4-3-3 killed the No.10.”**

The real change was deeper.

Football increasingly moved from thinking only about **positions** toward thinking about **functions**.

The old question was:

**“Who is our playmaker?”**

The answer might have been:

**“Our No.10.”**

The modern question is:

**“Who performs our playmaking functions?”**

And there may be several answers.

The centre-back.

The No.6.

The No.8.

The winger.

The False 9.

Even the full-back.

The playmaker stopped being only a **position**.

It became a **function**.

---

# 12. Özil — One of the Last Great Traditional No.10s

This transition brings us to a fascinating player.

**Mesut Özil.**

Özil is an excellent player for beginners to study because he represents many of the qualities associated with the traditional attacking playmaker.

His greatest weapons were not physical strength or defensive aggression.

They were:

**Vision.**

**Passing.**

**Movement between the lines.**

**The final ball.**

He could receive behind the opposition midfield and immediately find a forward runner.

```text
        Ronaldo
           ↑
           │
         Özil
        ↙    ↘
     PASS    PASS
```

This was classic No.10 football.

Find space.

Receive.

Turn.

See the run.

Deliver the final pass.

But players of this type increasingly faced another question.

Not:

**“How creative are you when your team has possession?”**

But:

**“What do you contribute when your team does not have possession?”**

That second question became increasingly important.

And it became one of the defining problems facing the classic No.10.

---

# 13. The Coach's Choice Changed

Imagine a coach choosing between two playmakers.

### PLAYER A — Classic No.10

Creativity ★★★★★
Passing ★★★★★
Technique ★★★★★
Pressing ★★
Defending ★
Work Rate ★★

### PLAYER B — Modern Playmaker

Creativity ★★★★
Passing ★★★★
Technique ★★★★
Pressing ★★★★★
Defending ★★★★
Work Rate ★★★★★

Player A might be the more gifted pure creator.

But Player B gives the coach more tactical possibilities.

When the team attacks:

**Playmaker.**

When possession is lost:

**Presser.**

When defending:

**Midfielder.**

When space opens:

**Runner into the box.**

One player performs several functions.

This quality has become increasingly valuable in modern football:

**Versatility.**

And this created a difficult question for the luxury No.10:

Why build an entire tactical system around a player who performs one phase brilliantly when another creative player can contribute to several phases?

---

# 14. So Is the No.10 Dead?

No.

This is the most important point of the entire episode.

**The No.10 did not die.**

The **old version of the No.10** became harder to accommodate.

The classic No.10 could almost say:

> Give me the ball.
> I will create the attack.

The modern playmaker has to say:

> I will find the space to receive.
> I will create the attack.
> I will attack the box.
> And if we lose possession, I will help win it back.

Creativity still exists.

What changed was the number of responsibilities attached to creativity.

---

# 15. So Where Did the No.10 Go?

The answer is surprisingly simple.

**Everywhere.**

```text
                 Classic No.10
                       │
          ┌────────────┼────────────┐
          ↓            ↓            ↓
   Creative No.8    False 9    Inverted Winger
          │                         │
          ↓                         ↓
Half-Space Creator          Hybrid Playmaker
```

The responsibilities once concentrated in one No.10 were redistributed across different positions.

This changes the question we should ask when watching modern football.

Don't ask:

**“Who is standing in the No.10 position?”**

Ask:

**“Who is performing the functions of a No.10?”**

That player may be standing much deeper.

Or wider.

Or even playing as the nominal striker.

---

# 16. Zidane → Totti → Kaká → Özil

Put these four players into a simplified timeline and the evolution becomes easier to understand.

### ZIDANE

**Control + Technique + Vision**

Receive under pressure, manipulate opponents and control space.

↓

### TOTTI

**Creativity + Goals**

Blur the boundary between playmaker and forward.

↓

### KAKÁ

**Creativity + Speed + Ball Carrying**

Don't just find the space.

**Attack it.**

↓

### ÖZIL

**Vision + Final Pass + Between-the-Lines Play**

A brilliant example of the central attacking playmaker surviving into the 2010s.

↓

### THE MODERN PLAYMAKER

Now add:

**Pressing + Mobility + Running + Versatility + Defensive Responsibility**

The transformation becomes clear.

---

# Conclusion — The No.10 Didn't Die. It Evolved.

So why did the No.10 we remember become increasingly rare?

Not because football stopped producing technically gifted players.

Not because creativity became less important.

**Football itself changed.**

Defensive structures became more compact.

The space between the lines became smaller.

Pressing became more aggressive.

Transitions became faster.

Attackers were expected to defend.

And playmaking responsibilities were distributed across multiple positions.

What disappeared was not:

**Creativity.**

What became increasingly difficult to accommodate was:

**Luxury.**

The player who could remain relatively free from defensive responsibilities.

The player whose primary responsibility was to create.

The player around whom the other ten players could be organized.

**The Luxury Playmaker.**

But the DNA of the No.10 survived.

Zidane's control.

Totti's goalscoring.

Kaká's forward runs.

Özil's final pass.

Those qualities did not disappear.

They migrated into new positions and new tactical roles.

That is why the story of the No.10 is not really a story about death.

**It is a story about evolution.**
