# EP03 — The Death of the Poacher

## Why Did Inzaghi-Type Goalscorers Disappear From Modern Football?

### The Evolution of Football Positions — No.9

There was once a very specific type of striker in football.

He might barely touch the ball for long periods.

He did not need to dribble constantly.

He did not have to drop into midfield.

He was not judged primarily on pressing intensity or build-up involvement.

But after 80 minutes, when a defender switched off for just one second, he appeared.

```text
Cross
  ↓
Deflection
  ↓
STRIKER
  ↓
GOAL
```

That player was the **poacher**.

Filippo Inzaghi is one of the clearest examples.

At first glance, his game could look strangely simple.

Few touches.

Few spectacular dribbles.

Little interest in controlling midfield.

But he constantly asked defenders one terrifying question:

> **“Do you know where I am right now?”**

The real skill of a poacher was not simply finishing.

It was knowing **where to be before the ball arrived**.

And yet today, pure poachers have almost disappeared from elite football.

Why?

Did the role become useless?

Or did modern tactics remove the conditions that allowed such players to survive?

The second explanation is more accurate.

# The poacher did not die.

# Being only a poacher stopped being enough.

---

# 1. The Poacher's Real Skill Was Not Shooting

A poacher is often described as someone who “just scores goals.”

That definition is too shallow.

The real craft is:

## Movement Before the Finish.

The best poachers do not wait for a chance.

They move to the place where the chance is about to appear.

```text
        CB        CB

             ST
```

An average striker sees the ball.

A poacher reads:

```text
BALL
 +
DEFENDER
 +
SPACE
 +
OFFSIDE LINE
```

When the defender looks toward the ball, the striker moves away from his vision.

```text
Defender → watches ball

                 ST
                  ↘
                   ↘
                 SPACE
```

The movement may only be two or three metres.

Inside the penalty area, that can be enough.

---

# 2. Inzaghi Attacked Attention, Not Just Space

It is common to say that Inzaghi had great positioning.

That is true, but incomplete.

More precisely, he attacked **defensive attention**.

A defender cannot perfectly watch both the ball and the striker at the same time.

```text
BALL  ← Defender → STRIKER
```

Look at the ball, and you risk losing the forward.

Look at the forward, and you may lose the flight or timing of the pass.

The poacher lives inside this problem.

When the ball moves wide:

```text
Winger ●
        \
         \
          CB    ST
```

the centre-back naturally turns toward the ball.

That is the trigger.

```text
Winger ●

          CB
             ST →
```

The striker moves onto the blind side.

By the time the cross arrives, the defender may already be beaten.

This is the true craft of the poacher.

---

# 3. Offside Was Not a Weakness — It Was the Cost of Risk

Players like Inzaghi were often caught offside.

From the outside, that could look inefficient.

But a poacher lives at the edge of the defensive line.

```text
DEFENSIVE LINE
──────────────

            ST
             ↑
       living on edge
```

He is constantly testing the limit.

```text
RUN 1 → Offside
RUN 2 → Offside
RUN 3 → Cleared
RUN 4 → Miss
RUN 5 → GOAL
```

Four failures can be forgotten if the fifth run decides the match.

That was the bargain.

The poacher accepted repeated failure because one successful movement could outweigh everything else.

---

# 4. Old Football Protected the Poacher

Why could these players survive at the highest level?

Because the team did not ask them to do everything.

Their job was clear.

```text
TEAM
 ↓
CREATE CHANCE
 ↓
POACHER
 ↓
FINISH
```

The midfield progressed the ball.

The winger crossed.

The No.10 created the final pass.

The second striker could link play.

The poacher finished.

The system protected his weaknesses and maximized his strongest quality.

---

# 5. Two-Striker Systems Were Perfect for Poachers

The two-striker structure was especially helpful.

```text
        POACHER       SECOND STRIKER
           ●                ●

        Stay High          Drop Deep
        Finish             Create
        Attack Box         Link Play
```

One forward could come toward the ball.

The other could stay close to goal.

One could create.

The other could finish.

This meant the poacher did not need to become a midfielder.

He could remain what he was:

## a specialist in the final action.

---

# 6. The Lone-Striker Era Changed the Equation

Modern football gradually moved toward systems with one central forward.

```text
LW              CF              RW
```

Now the CF had to do far more.

Tasks once divided between two forwards were placed on one player.

```text
CF
 ↓
Finish
Press
Link
Hold
Run
Create Space
```

This was the first major crisis for the pure poacher.

# Goals were still important.

# But goals alone were no longer enough.

---

# 7. The First Problem: Pressing

In modern football, the striker is often the first defender.

When the opponent builds from the back:

```text
CB1          CB2
      DM
```

the centre-forward cannot simply wait near the halfway line.

He has defensive responsibilities.

```text
          CF
         ↙

CB1          CB2
      DM
```

He may need to press the centre-back while using his body to block the passing lane into midfield.

That requires understanding:

Pressing angles.

Triggers.

Cover shadows.

Passing lanes.

Defensive timing.

This is very different from the old poacher's job.

---

# 8. The Second Problem: Build-Up Play

Modern teams also involve their striker in possession.

```text
CB → CM → CF
```

The striker may receive with his back to goal.

Protect the ball.

Lay it off first time.

Find a midfielder.

Release a winger.

Create a third-man combination.

```text
        CF
        ↓
 receive between lines

LW      AM      RW
```

The striker now needs to provide value outside the penalty area.

That is a major problem for a player whose entire identity is based on waiting for the final action.

---

# 9. The Value of Every Touch Increased

In older systems, a striker could have very few touches and still have a successful match.

One goal could justify everything.

Modern football is less forgiving.

A poor touch in build-up can immediately become:

```text
TURNOVER
   ↓
COUNTERATTACK
```

So coaches increasingly demand:

First touch.

Ball retention.

Link play.

Decision-making.

Passing quality.

A striker now participates in the team's structure with almost every action.

That makes the “just score” specialist much harder to accommodate.

---

# 10. The Rise of the Inside Forward

Another major change was the movement of goals from the centre to the wings.

Traditionally:

```text
WINGER → CROSS → POACHER → GOAL
```

Then wide attackers became major goalscorers.

```text
WINGER
   ↓
CUT INSIDE
   ↓
SHOOT
   ↓
GOAL
```

Cristiano Ronaldo, Lionel Messi and Arjen Robben helped redefine what a wide forward could be.

Now the winger was not simply supplying the striker.

He could be the team's main scorer.

This changed the centre-forward's role.

Sometimes the CF's job became:

```text
CF ↓

CB follows
   ↓

SPACE → WINGER RUN
```

The striker could create space for somebody else to score.

That is almost the opposite of the traditional poacher.

---

# 11. The False 9 Was the Ultimate Challenge

Then came an even more radical idea.

The False 9.

The traditional poacher stays close to the defensive line.

```text
CB      ST      CB
```

The False 9 moves away from it.

```text
CB              CB

        ↓
       F9

    MIDFIELD
```

Now the centre-backs face a dilemma.

Follow him:

```text
CB ↓ follows F9

SPACE ↑
     WINGER
```

and space opens behind.

Stay:

```text
F9 receives free
between the lines
```

and the False 9 receives between midfield and defence.

This was a completely different interpretation of the centre-forward position.

The traditional poacher waited for space.

The False 9 **created space by leaving it**.

---

# 12. Messi Changed the Standard

Lionel Messi became the clearest symbol of this revolution.

He could start centrally but behave nothing like a traditional striker.

```text
Messi
  ↓
drop

Xavi / Iniesta
  ↔
Messi
```

He moved into midfield.

Combined with teammates.

Dribbled.

Created chances.

Pulled defenders out of position.

Then attacked the box again.

And scored.

This forced coaches to rethink the position.

The question became:

> **“Why should the centre-forward stay between the centre-backs at all?”**

That question was devastating for the pure poacher.

---

# 13. Defensive Blocks Became More Compact

Modern defending also became more collective.

The back line moves together.

```text
LB ─ CB ─ CB ─ RB
     ↑ compact ↑
```

And midfield protects the space ahead.

```text
      DM
   CM    CM

LB CB  CB RB
```

This makes central spaces much harder to access.

If a striker simply waits between the centre-backs:

```text
      DM

   CB ST CB
```

the ball may never reach him.

So the modern striker must move.

Drop.

Drift wide.

Pull defenders away.

Rotate.

Then attack the box again.

Waiting is no longer enough.

---

# 14. High Defensive Lines Created a New Kind of Poacher

High defensive lines did create one opportunity.

There is more space behind.

```text
HIGH LINE
──────────

       SPACE
       SPACE
       SPACE
```

But attacking that space requires different qualities.

Acceleration.

Timing.

Transition speed.

Ball carrying.

One-v-one ability.

So the modern penalty-box hunter increasingly became:

```text
POACHER
  +
SPRINTER
  +
RUNNER
```

The poacher did not disappear.

He evolved.

---

# 15. VAR Made the Margins Even Smaller

Poachers have always lived on the edge of offside.

Modern VAR technology has made that edge far less forgiving.

A run that once looked perfectly timed can now become:

```text
Perfect-looking run
        ↓
VAR
        ↓
Offside
```

VAR did not kill the poacher.

But it increased the precision required for players whose entire game is based on manipulating the last defensive line.

The margin became even smaller.

---

# 16. Modern Coaches Want More Than “Invisible for 80 Minutes”

The classic description of a poacher could sound like this:

> “He did nothing for 80 minutes — then he scored.”

In another era, that could still be a successful performance.

Today, coaches ask more questions.

Did he press?

Did he block the opposition No.6?

Did he protect the ball?

Did he connect midfield and attack?

Did he create space for wide players?

Did he help the team counter-press?

Did he make runs in transition?

The centre-forward is now judged across the full 90 minutes.

Not only at the moment of the finish.

---

# 17. So Did Poaching Disappear?

No.

This distinction is crucial.

The **skill of poaching** is still essential.

Near-post runs.

Far-post runs.

Rebounds.

Blind-side movement.

Second balls.

One-touch finishing.

These remain fundamental striker skills.

What disappeared was not:

# Poaching.

What disappeared was:

# The Poacher-Only Player.

---

# 18. Modern Elite Strikers Still Become Poachers in the Box

The modern striker performs different roles in different phases.

```text
Build-Up Phase
   ↓
Link Player

Pressing Phase
   ↓
First Defender

Transition
   ↓
Runner

Penalty Area
   ↓
POACHER
```

That is the key difference.

He is not a poacher for the entire game.

But when the ball enters the penalty area, he still needs the instincts of one.

---

# 19. Haaland Is a Modern Example

Erling Haaland is a good example.

He is often described in terms of size, speed and power.

But much of his danger comes from penalty-area movement.

He might begin behind a defender.

```text
CB      ST
```

Then suddenly attack the front.

Or begin in front and disappear onto the blind side.

```text
CB → watches ball

             ST →
```

Near post.

Far post.

Cutback zone.

Rebound zone.

Those movements are pure poacher behaviour.

The difference is that the modern version also brings:

Power.

Acceleration.

Depth running.

Transition threat.

Pressing.

The old skill remains.

The total package is larger.

---

# 20. Lewandowski Shows the Same Evolution

Robert Lewandowski combines old and new striker qualities.

Inside the box, he can:

Pin the centre-back.

Attack the blind side.

Time a cross.

Finish first time.

Read rebounds.

But outside the box, he can also:

Receive under pressure.

Protect possession.

Link with midfield.

Combine with teammates.

Participate in pressing.

This is the modern formula.

```text
OLD-SCHOOL POACHER
        +
MODERN LINK PLAYER
        =
COMPLETE No.9
```

---

# 21. The Poacher Was Not Killed — He Was Absorbed

This is the best way to understand the evolution.

In the past:

```text
POACHER
=
PLAYER IDENTITY
```

Today:

```text
POACHER
=
ONE PHASE
OF THE STRIKER'S GAME
```

That is the real change.

A striker can no longer simply say:

**“I am a poacher.”**

He increasingly needs to say:

**“I am a pressing forward, a link player, a channel runner — and when I enter the box, I become a poacher.”**

---

# 22. From Specialist to Multi-Functional Forward

The poacher's evolution is part of a much larger story.

```text
SPECIALIST
   ↓
HYBRID
   ↓
MULTI-FUNCTIONAL PLAYER
```

The same thing happened elsewhere.

The classic No.10 changed.

The winger changed.

The full-back changed.

The goalkeeper changed.

Modern football increasingly asks one player to perform functions that once belonged to several different positions.

The poacher is one of the clearest examples.

---

---

# Conclusion — The Poacher Is Not Dead

Why do we see fewer Inzaghi-type forwards today?

Not because their abilities became useless.

In fact, the best qualities of the classic poacher are still essential:

Positioning.

Blind-side movement.

Offside-line timing.

Rebound anticipation.

One-touch finishing.

What changed was the size of the role.

In the past:

```text
POACHER
      =
FULL-TIME JOB
```

Today:

```text
POACHER
      =
PART OF THE JOB
```

That is why pure poachers have become rare.

But look closely at the world's best strikers in decisive moments and you will still see the same old instincts.

The defender turns toward the ball.

The blind side opens.

The cross is about to arrive.

The goalkeeper spills the first shot.

And the striker moves.

One step.

Two metres.

Half a second.

Goal.

## The poacher is not dead.

## He has been absorbed into the modern No.9.
