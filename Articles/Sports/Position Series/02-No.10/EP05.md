# EP05 — Is the No.10 Really Dead?

## Bruno, Musiala, Wirtz, Palmer — A New Way to Understand the Modern Playmaker

Is the No.10 really dead?

Modern football gives us a complicated answer.

Bruno Fernandes still attempts the killer passes we associate with a No.10.

Jamal Musiala receives between the lines and breaks defensive structures.

Florian Wirtz connects midfield with attack.

Cole Palmer creates chances while also scoring himself.

Jude Bellingham starts in midfield and arrives inside the box.

They all carry something from the old No.10.

But none of them plays exactly the same way.

So instead of asking:

**"Who is the No.10?"**

Modern football gives us a better question:

**"Who performs each playmaking function?"**

---

# 1. Look at Function, Not Just Position

In the past, position and responsibility were easier to connect.

```text id="32pqch"
          ST

        No.10
```

The No.10 played behind the striker and carried much of the team's creative responsibility.

Modern creativity is more complicated.

```text id="mzcl3r"
              ST

      LW              RW

         No.8     No.8

              DM
```

The player creating chances does not always stand in the centre.

He might operate in the left half-space.

He might start on the right wing and move inside.

He might begin deeper in midfield.

He might even attack space without touching the ball.

So to understand the modern playmaker, we need to look beyond position.

We need to look at **Function**.

---

# 2. Bruno Fernandes — The Risk Creator

The strongest piece of traditional No.10 DNA in Bruno Fernandes is his willingness to **take risks**.

He is not a player who always searches for the safest pass.

```text id="m1c8zn"
Receive
   ↓
Look Forward
   ↓
Through Ball / Cross / Shot
```

If he sees space behind the defence, he tries to exploit it.

If a forward makes a run, he looks for the pass.

If shooting space appears, he can attack the goal himself.

Naturally, this style can also lead to lost possession.

But that is part of the role.

**Accept Risk → Create Reward**

### Bruno's Playmaking Function

**Chance Creation**

The aggressive passing instinct of the classic No.10 survives inside a modern midfielder.

---

# 3. Musiala — The Space Breaker

Jamal Musiala creates differently.

His playmaking often begins with **dribbling** rather than passing.

Imagine a compact defensive block.

```text id="h7a3wx"
   DF      DF
      DM

    MUSIALA
```

A traditional playmaker might search for a pass through that structure.

Musiala can choose another solution.

He enters the space himself.

```text id="td2s3e"
DF       DF
   ↘   ↙
  MUSIALA
      ↓
    BREAK
```

Beat one defender and another must react.

When the second defender moves, space opens somewhere else.

That means Musiala's dribbling is not simply individual skill.

It can become a form of **playmaking through disruption**.

### Musiala's Playmaking Function

**Space Breaking**

He does not only find space.

**He creates it.**

---

# 4. Wirtz — The Connector

Florian Wirtz represents another type of creativity.

His strength is connecting different parts of the attack.

```text id="nht5ve"
Midfield
    ↓
  WIRTZ
 ↙     ↘
LW     ST
```

Receive.

Combine.

Move.

Receive again.

The important action is not always one spectacular final pass.

It is keeping the attack connected as it moves toward goal.

### Wirtz's Playmaking Function

**Connection**

He can become the bridge between midfield and attack.

So when watching a player like Wirtz, assists alone do not tell the whole story.

A better question is:

**How does the attack flow through him?**

---

# 5. Palmer — The Creator-Finisher

Cole Palmer represents another important evolution.

Traditionally, the responsibilities could be relatively clear:

```text id="x9r3ya"
No.10 → CREATE

ST → FINISH
```

A player like Palmer can combine both.

```text id="tgnlwy"
          PALMER
         ↙      ↘
     CREATE    SCORE
```

Protect against his pass and he can attack the goal himself.

Step toward his shot and another passing option can appear.

The defender now has two problems.

### Palmer's Playmaking Function

**Create + Finish**

The modern attacking playmaker does not have to be an assist specialist.

He can also be one of the team's main scoring threats.

---

# 6. Bellingham — The Arriver

Jude Bellingham influences attacks in a very different way.

His value is not always about where he receives the ball.

Sometimes the key question is:

**When does he arrive?**

```text id="s6rsh3"
        ST
        ↓
      Defence

       SPACE
         ↑
    BELLINGHAM
```

The striker occupies the centre-backs.

The defenders focus on the forward line and the ball.

Then Bellingham arrives from midfield.

This is the power of the **Late Arrival**.

The classic No.10 often started in the dangerous space behind the striker.

Bellingham can begin deeper and attack that space later.

### Bellingham's Playmaking Function

**Progress + Arrive**

He does not simply occupy attacking space.

**He attacks it from midfield.**

---

# 7. Olmo — The Hybrid

Dani Olmo is difficult to define with one fixed position.

That is exactly what makes him interesting.

He can receive between the lines.

Move toward the wing.

Combine in the half-space.

Or enter the penalty area.

```text id="eexfgo"
      ST

   OLMO → → RW
     ↓
 Half-Space
     ↓
    BOX
```

For defenders, his next position is not always predictable.

### Olmo's Playmaking Function

**Interchange**

The modern playmaker does not always need one perfect position.

He can perform similar creative functions from **multiple positions**.

---

# 8. Szoboszlai — The Accelerator

Dominik Szoboszlai brings another quality:

**Speed of attack.**

Receive.

Turn.

Run or pass forward.

```text id="3p7rfn"
Receive
   ↓
Turn
   ↓
RUN / PASS
   ↓
Attack Accelerates
```

His directness can reduce the amount of time the opposition has to reorganize.

This reveals another form of playmaking.

Not every creator needs to slow the game down and control possession.

Some creators are dangerous because they do the opposite.

### Szoboszlai's Playmaking Function

**Acceleration**

Some playmakers control the game by slowing it down.

Others break it open by making it faster.

---

# 9. Fermín — The Space Arriver

Fermín López offers an especially interesting modern interpretation.

With him, the important question is often not:

**"Where can he receive?"**

It is:

**"Where can he arrive?"**

```text id="n2b9lc"
       ST
       ↓
   CB      CB

      SPACE
        ↑
      FERMÍN
```

He attacks dangerous spaces without the ball.

Those movements create passing options for the player in possession and problems for defenders.

This means a player can contribute to creation without always being the one making the creative pass.

### Fermín's Playmaking Function

**Off-Ball Space Attack**

This is an important idea in modern football:

**Creativity can exist without the ball.**

---

# 10. A Better Map of the Modern Playmaker

Calling all of these players "No.10s" hides their differences.

Looking at their functions makes the picture much clearer.

| Player              | Main Function    | How He Creates                   |
| ------------------- | ---------------- | -------------------------------- |
| **Bruno Fernandes** | Risk Creator     | Forward passes / chance creation |
| **Musiala**         | Space Breaker    | Dribbling                        |
| **Wirtz**           | Connector        | Movement + combinations          |
| **Palmer**          | Creator-Finisher | Passing + goal threat            |
| **Bellingham**      | Arriver          | Progression + late runs          |
| **Olmo**            | Hybrid           | Positional interchange           |
| **Szoboszlai**      | Accelerator      | Vertical running + tempo         |
| **Fermín**          | Space Arriver    | Off-ball movement                |

The point is not to decide which player is better.

The point is that they solve attacking problems in **different ways**.

---

# 11. So, Is the No.10 Really Dead?

Now we can return to the question that started this series.

**Is the No.10 dead?**

As a fixed position, perhaps partially.

The era when one player could remain centrally behind the striker and dominate almost all of the team's creative responsibility has faded.

But as a function?

The answer is very different.

```text id="54j5t9"
Classic No.10
      ↓
  Creativity
      ↓
 ┌────┼────┬──────┐
 ↓    ↓    ↓      ↓
No.8 Wing Half-Space Forward
```

The old No.10's abilities did not disappear.

They were **distributed across different players and different spaces**.

Vision survived.

The final pass survived.

Dribbling survived.

Between-the-lines play survived.

Goalscoring survived.

Movement survived.

The difference is that one player no longer needs to own all of them.

---

# Conclusion — The No.10 Became a Language

The No.10 was once a position.

It was also an identity.

A special player standing behind the striker, given responsibility for imagination.

Modern football allows us to see it differently.

The No.10 is no longer just a coordinate on a tactical board.

It has become something closer to a **football language**.

Bruno speaks it through passing.

Musiala through dribbling.

Wirtz through connection.

Palmer through creation and finishing.

Bellingham through arrival.

Olmo through movement between positions.

Szoboszlai through acceleration.

Fermín through movement without the ball.

They are very different players.

But traces of the No.10 still exist inside all of them.

So after five episodes, we can finally answer the question:

**Is the No.10 dead?**

**The position faded.
The function survived.**

The No.10 did not die.

**It evolved from one player into many functions.**
